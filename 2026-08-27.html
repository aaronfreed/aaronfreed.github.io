When I got tickets to see the Los Angeles Dodgers play against the Atlanta Braves on August 27, 2026, at the latter’s home field of Truist Park, I hoped merely to see a good ball game. The Braves and Dodgers are my two favorite baseball teams, but at the time, the Braves were coming off a disappointing 2024 and an abysmal 2025 that certainly gave off the appearance of being a rebuild. Of course, the Dodgers were the defending back-to-back World Series winners, so I figured at least I’d see great performances from them.

(I still, to this very day, feel about 1991’s World Series the way Toronto Blue Jays fans must feel about 2025’s. Yes, arguably the greatest pitcher’s duel in World Series history; yes, Jack Morris threw the game of his career in the highest-pressure environment possible. I’ve heard all the arguments, and I’m still not over it; even seeing replays of it still hurts. I also haven’t forgiven the Blue Jays for ’92 or the Phillies for, well, anything.)

In any case, I needn’t have worried: the Braves turned out to be one of Major League Baseball’s best teams in 2026 (they’d just been incredibly unlucky with injuries in 2025), so I became increasingly optimistic that I’d see great performances from both teams. Once the starting pitchers were announced – 2025 World Series MVP Yoshinobu Yamamoto for the Dodgers, 2024 Cy Young Award winner Chris Sale for the Braves – I began to hope to see one of the best pitchers’ duels of the year. I expected neither team to score more than two runs, and given both starters’ tendency to go deep into games, I began to hope I might even see a complete game from one or both of them. I wasn’t so greedy as to tempt the baseball gods by hoping to see a complete-game shutout.

That’s exactly what I saw, though, and that’s only the start of it. I’ve attended hundreds of baseball games in my time. This was by far the best of them. It was also undoubtedly one of the best regular-season games of the year, and it capped off one of the best regular-season series of the year. The Braves took all three games by one-run margins, but all three were substantially different.

The only scoring in this game happened on the very first pitch Yamamoto threw: Braves catcher Drake Baldwin knocked one out of the park. This is not unusual for Yamamoto: he has a tendency to give up bombs to the leadoff batter and then lock in for the rest of the game. The most noteworthy example of this happened in game two of last year’s National League Championship Series, when he gave up a leadoff tank to Milwaukee Brewers right fielder Jackson Chourio and proceeded to throw a complete game. The Dodgers won 5-1 and went on to sweep the series.

Of course, Yamamoto didn’t get the same kind of run support here. He was nails, though: he threw 105 pitches across six and one-third innings without giving up another run, struck out eight batters, walked only one, and allowed only four hits. Against a lesser pitcher, he’d have been assured a victory.

Baldwin’s leadoff home run was quite unusual. Since 1900, only 33 players have hit leadoff home runs in 1-0 victories. Baldwin is the first Braves player and the first catcher ever to do so in that time.

A familial connection

I may have buried the lede on Chris Sale. He’s my second cousin. As a result, I’d resolved before the game to root for the Braves as long as Sale was on the mound and root merely to see a good ball game after he left.

Which he never did. He threw the ninth complete-game shutout by any pitcher this season on 109 pitches, striking out eleven batters and allowing only five hits and no bases on balls (although he did hit two batters, which is hardly an unusual occurrence for him: he leads MLB in hit-by-pitches during the years he’s been active).

80 (73.4%) of Sale’s pitches were strikes; he threw a 100.1 mph fastball to Shohei Ohtani in the third inning (his fastest pitch since he threw 100.3 mph on 2018-07-16 and his seventh-fastest of his career) and struck out Alex Call on his final pitch of the game with 99 mph. In short, it was a career game for him – perhaps the game of his career.

Facing the GOAT

On his uptick in velocity against Ohtani, Sale said in an interview on The Pat McAfee Show the following day, “Yeah, well, I dare you to go throw a get-me-over fastball to Shohei. He’d probably hit that thing to the freaking Comcast building. So yeah, I think the game will dictate and tell you when it’s time to bear down and dig in a little bit, and I think given the circumstances, when you have a generational guy like he is – because I mean, let’s face it, he’s kind of the gold standard guy right now of of what it is to be just an ultimate baseball player, right? I mean, he’s doing things no one’s ever seen, and you get in those situations, and it’s a tight game. You talk about atmosphere and adrenaline. Adrenaline is dictated by moments and energy in the crowd. And if you’ve got 3,000 fans there that aren’t really paying attention to the game, the adrenaline is not going to be the same as last night, you got a packed house, our fans are going crazy. You can feel that. You might not see it or hear it ornotice it, but there’s a certain feeling. I’m sure you know what I’m talking about, but there’s certain times in where you’ve got to step up.”

He’s not wrong, which makes it even more impressive that he struck out Ohtani twice. No one has done anything like what Ohtani’s been doing in the integration era (1947-present) for as long as he’s done it. Several two-way players have been inducted into Cooperstown over the years, but they all either:

  • played in the 19th century (Albert Spalding, John Montgomery Ward, Old Hoss Radbourn),
  • played in the Negro Leagues (Bullet Rogan, Martín Díhigo, Ben Taylor, Cool Papa Bell, Ray Brown, etc.), and/or
  • only served as two-way players for at most two seasons (Bobby Wallace in 1896, George Sisler in 1915, Babe Ruth in 1918-1919, Jimmie Foxx in 1945).

As much as people compare Ohtani to Ruth, the Bambino wasn’t facing 100.1 mph heaters, and he was only truly a two-way player for two years. Shohei has already done it full-time for three full seasons (2021-2023) and parts of three more (2018, 2025-2026).

(Admittedly, Ohtani hasn’t been quite himself lately, but his first half of the season may have been the best half-season we’ve ever seen from anyone. As of June 24, 81 games into the season, he was hitting .293/.414/.552, with a league-leading .966 OPS and 169 OPS, and he’d pitched to a 1.47 ERA, 277 ERA+, 2.66 FIP, and 0.882 WHIP in 79⅔ IP, leaving him a meager 1⅓ IP from qualifying for the ERA title; he’d have been a close second-place in several of those stats if he had.)

Milestones

When Braves broadcaster Wiley Ballard asked Sale how he threw a 99 mph fastball for his 109th pitch of the game, Sale had a perfect answer: “With my arm.” That’s Chris Sale for you: his velocity may rival that of many other modern starters, but he’s an old-school hurler of the sort you rarely see these days. As of September 2, 2026, he has 177 strikeouts in 2026 and 2,756 across his career, leaving him well poised to become the 21st and very likely final pitcher to reach 3,000 career strikeouts – he’ll need slightly more than another full season like this one to do so. Assuming he does so, he will become only the fifth left-handed member of the club, alongside future Hall of Famer Clayton Kershaw and current Hall of Famers Steve Carlton, CC Sabathia, and Randy Johnson (Sale’s childhood idol).

Sale’s shutout at 37 years and 150 days of age also made him the oldest pitcher to throw a complete-game shutout with no bases on balls and at least ten strikeouts since Johnson shut out the Braves on May 18, 2004, at the age of 40 years and 190 days. According to MLB reporter Sarah Langs on Bluesky, only six other pitchers Sale’s age or older had accomplished the feat since at least 1900, and two of them were Johnson (he also pulled off the feat in 2001). The other four were Nolan Ryan (1989), Steve Carlton (1982), Eddie Plank (1913), and Cy Young (1905).

Sale is also tied with Roger Clemens for the sixth-most such games since the mound was moved to its current distance in 1893, behind Johnson (36), Max Scherzer (31), Curt Schilling (27), Clayton Kershaw (27), and Gerrit Cole (24).

Sale’s 95 career 10+ strikeout games rank seventh since 1893, behind Ryan (215), Johnson (212), Scherzer (114), Clemens (110), Pedro Martínez (108), and Sandy Koufax (97). He’s the fourth-oldest Brave to throw a shutout in the last forty years, behind John Smoltz (38 years, 37 days), Smoltz (38 years, 335 days), and Dennis Martinez (44 years, 19 days), and he’s one of only two pitchers to throw 100 mph or faster at age 37 or older in the pitch-tracking era (2008-present), alongside Jacob deGrom.

Chris is having an incredible back half of the year. In the span from July 16 (the All-Star Break) to September 6, he’s led MLB in FanGraphs WAR (2.8), BB/9 IP (0.69), HR/9 IP (0.17), and FIP (1.23). A 1.23 FIP is the sort of thing we normally only see from relievers. Jacob Misiorowski is still the presumptive NL Cy Young favorite, but Sale has pretty securely unseated Cristopher Sánchez as the runner-up, and should Sale and Misiorowski’s last six weeks be indicative of how the rest of their seasons will go, this race could end up a lot closer than we thought.

At this point, Chris Sale could retire at the end of this season and be a Hall of Famer, and probably a first-ballot Hall of Famer at that. The only lingering question is how much of the vote he’ll get.

The 3,000-strikeout club

To be clear, I’m certain he’ll continue pitching for at least another two seasons, because a certain trait runs in our family – call it perseverance or, if you like, call it stubbornness; perhaps they’re the same thing. As of this writing, Chris has 2,763 career strikeouts. When we get within striking distance of a milestone as monumental as 3,000 strikeouts, we keep going until we reach it.

Only 20 pitchers have reached that milestone. Of them, Clayton Kershaw will be inducted into Cooperstown in 2031, Justin Verlander in 2032, and Max Scherzer five years after his retirement (these aren’t predictions; they’re simple statements of fact), and all but two of the others already have been. Only four members of the club are lefties: Randy Johnson, Steve Carlton, Clayton Kershaw, and CC Sabathia.

Chris Sale will be the 3,000-strikeout club’s twenty-first member. This, too, is a statement of fact. Moreover, he may be its last. He’s third place on MLB’s active strikeout leaders list behind Verlander and Scherzer. I intend no shade on any of the following pitchers, but I’m less convinced that any of them will make it than that Sale will:

  • Fourth-place Gerrit Cole, who’ll turn 36 on September 8, has 2,364, which means he’d either need to keep pitching until he was at least 40, or he’d need to strike out at least 200 batters in his next three seasons. The latter isn’t outside the realm of possibility if he stays healthy, but that’s a big “if”: he missed 2025 due to Tommy John surgery.
  • Fifth-place Kevin Gausman, also currently 35 years old, has 2,119. He hasn’t had a 200-strikeout season since 2023. He could do it, but it’s a long shot.
  • Sixth-place Yu Darvish, age 39, has 2,075.
  • Seventh-place Sonny Gray, age 36, has 2,060.
  • Eighth-place Aaron Nola, age 33, has 2,038. He could have a shot if he stays healthy.

  • Ninth-place Jacob deGrom, age 38, has 2,025. Had he not had such awful injury luck, he’d probably already be a member.

    deGrom’s Hall of Fame case will also be an interesting bellwether of how future starting pitchers will be treated. Were it entirely up to me, he’d be inducted on the first ballot, but he won’t have a lot of the counting stats that the BBWAA seems to love so much. Arguably no one in his generation had better pure stuff than he did at his peak, though. He also won Rookie of the Year and two Cy Young Awards, and he’s second-place all-time in WHIP, behind only Hall of Famer Addie Joss and just ahead of Hall of Famer Ed Walsh. And his ERA, ERA+, FIP, and WHIP have led MLB across the time he’s been active – in short, all the most important rate stats.

    (Unrelatedly, I also wonder if he could’ve made it in MLB as a two-way player, considering he was no slouch as a shortstop in college – indeed, he once went yard against Sale, to which Sale himself said, “Don’t let him lie to you: that ball was crushed. Oh my god, crushed. And he did it batting lefty.”)

Of the remaining people on the list, I think the likeliest to make a serious run at 3,000 is 21st-place Dylan Cease, age 30, with 1,452 Ks. Cease leads the American League in Ks per 9 IP this year, with 12.7, and his 221 strikeouts after 144 games puts him on pace for a 249-strikeout season. He’ll probably slow down somewhat as he ages, but if anything, he’s ramping up; his previous season best was 227, and he’s already almost passed that. Although Cam Schlittler is considered the AL Cy Young favorite, Cease is providing his stiffest competition.

The inevitable question of how younger pitchers like Misiorowski may fare depends on their health.

  • The Miz, age 24, has 227 strikeouts this year, putting him on pace for a 255-strikeout season, but it’s unlikely he’ll maintain that pace across an entire career. (His career total is 314.)
  • In what’s otherwise a down year for him, Paul Skenes, also age 24, still has 181 strikeouts on the year, putting him on pace to surpass 200 strikeouts, if just barely. (His career total is 567.)
  • Yamamoto, at age 28, has 161 strikeouts this year (a pace of 181) and 467 in his MLB career. (He had 922 in seven NPB seasons, which raises the interesting question of how many he’d have if he’d moved to MLB sooner. Between MLB and NPB, he’s struck out 1,389 batters.)

Pitchers like Misiorowski, Yamamoto, and Skenes could make serious runs at 3,000 *if* they stay healthy and *if* their managers use them enough to give them a serious chance. The issue is that, on the whole, pitchers aren’t going as deep into games, and they’re getting injured more often, so the days of pitchers seeing enough playing time to even reach the milestone may be over.

Take Blake Snell, the all-time leader in K/9 IP, who’s in 18th place with 1,483 Ks. Snell probably could’ve made a serious run at it if the Dodgers hadn’t been so focused on keeping him rested for the postseason – he’s on pace to average about 50 IP per season when this season concludes. He’s been nails this year in particular, pitching to a 1.97 ERA, 212 ERA+, 1.82 FIP, and 1.092 WHIP, but he’s only thrown 32 innings to date.

(Yamamoto may actually be the most interesting outlier here, due to his unusual training retinue focused more on flexibility and endurance than on strength. This attracted some attention last year after he won World Series MVP, but it remains to be seen how much it will catch on. Thus far, he has yet to be sidelined for more than a few months by an injury.)

For a long time, people cited Zack Greinke, Clayton Kershaw, Justin Verlander, and Max Scherzer as their generation’s four starting pitchers who were certain to be enshrined in Cooperstown. At this point, it’s hard to imagine a defensible argument against adding Chris Sale to their ranks. In many ways, he is the last of a dying breed.

On family

Sale mentioned in an interview with Pat McAfee the day after the game that he’d played catch with his sons earlier that day. I believe him. But also, excuse me, literally how? You hit 100 mph last night, you threw a 109-pitch shutout against the defending back-to-back World Series champions the night before, you’re 37 years old, and you’re still good to play catch? I’d have been too sore to get out of bed. He must have an endurance to rival Yamamoto’s. (To be clear, I absolutely believe him. For those unaware, Yamamoto earned his World Series MVP in part by throwing 2⅔ shutout innings to close Game 7 after having thrown six innings of one-run ball in Game 6 the night prior. And just for good measure, he’d thrown one-run complete games in Game 2 of both the World Series and the NLCS, and he was warming up in the bullpen in Game 3 when former Braves legend Freddie Freeman hit a walkoff home run in the eighteenth inning. Some guys are just built different.)

When Pat asked Chris about being an elder statesman, he was remarkably humble and spent most of his answer talking about his own mentors:

“There’s this certain brotherhood, this certain umbrella that’s over all of us. I just think back when I was a kid, and I remember at my first All-Star Game, walking in there, and I was in the corner with pitchers like Justin Verlander and CC Sabathia and Jered Weaver and Fernando Rodney, and I remember just being like, ‘Man, these are the guys I grew up watching, and now I’m in the same locker room, just sitting there, chopping it up, talking baseball.’ I think that’s a very important aspect of the game that shouldn’t be lost, just little things that get passed down from generation to generation. I’m doing things that I learned from the guys before me. And I hope maybe there’s something that I do or something that I say that resonates with somebody, and they can use it, and then they pass it on. That’s a cool, cool thing.

“Like A.J. Pierzinski was at the game last night, and he was my first catcher in the big leagues, and he was like, ‘Man, I’m just glad you made it quick.’ And I said, ‘You know, that’s something I learned from Mark Burleigh,’ and I’m hoping maybe in ten years from now, there’s a guy that learns that from me, but I learned it from him. And then you got, thirty or forty years down the line, and Mark Burleigh is still a name getting thrown around because of something I did that’s something he taught me, and something someone taught him before.

“So this game can be special that way. And again, like I said, it’s a certain brotherhood that you just try to keep connected. But once those lights flick on, it’s go time.”

Pat also mentioned that that no one else in MLB history had pulled off across their entire careers what Chris had done the previous night:

  • Complete-game shutout versus the back-to-back defending World Series champs
  • Complete-game shutout against a lineup including three former MVPs (Ohtani, Freeman, and Mookie Betts)
  • Complete-game shutout with a reigning World Series MVP (Yamamoto) as the opposing starting pitcher
  • Complete-game shutout with 10+ strikeouts and zero bases on balls
  • 1-0 complete-game shutout victory

(Said lineup also included the prior year’s NLCS MVP [Ohtani] and three players who’d be first-ballot Hall of Famers if they retired tomorrow [again, Ohtani, Freeman, and Betts].)

Chris responded far more humbly than I would’ve: “They like to cherry-pick those, with the sun setting at a certain time south of the Mason-Dixon line with 46% humidity and elevation here and there. I mean, I do appreciate it. I do love the history of the game, but you can get down a rabbit hole chasing numbers sometimes.”

In a separate interview, he told The Athletic:

“I pride myself on going out there and giving my team a chance to win. Sometimes it’s good, sometimes it’s not. But I’m just thankful for the people that I have in my corner to help me get out to that mound. I’ve said it a lot, but this isn’t me just behind the scenes doing what I do to get out there. I’ve got a lot of people in my corner helping me out.…

“I hope I’ve got some coming. It was awesome. I definitely appreciate it. I’m thankful for it. But at the end of the day, it still counts as one win.”

I’m in awe of Chris Sale’s pitching ability, but it’s not just that. A team player needs to be able to lift others up (a rising tide lifts all boats, as they say) and to give credit where it is due. Chris does an exemplary job of both of those, and he clearly hasn’t let fame or success get to his head.

Politics and team sports are nothing alike

A lot of people claim that American politics has come increasingly to resemble team sports. These people are telling us they understand nothing about team sports without telling us they understand nothing about team sports. When Chris came out for the ninth inning, and again after he completed the game, the entire stadium, Dodgers fans included, got to their feet in recognition of a monumental achievement.

This isn’t a unique occurrence – visiting pitchers have routinely gotten standing ovations from home crowds for throwing no-hitters or perfect games. Indeed, earlier this year, Yamamoto got a standing ovation from the crowd at the Chicago White Sox’ Rate Field when he exited after his eight-inning no-hit bid was broken up in the ninth by a solo home run. (Yamamoto still got the victory, as the Dodgers won that game 7-1.)

Likewise, when the Miami Marlins removed pitcher Eury Pérez after throwing seven perfect innings earlier this year, they were treated to a chorus of boos and “Shame” chants from the Oakland Sacramento Athletics of Las Vegas’ home crowd, despite their team being down 8-0 at the time. (The Fish would go on to win this game, but only by a 9-8 margin.)

Put another way, there is a level of, well, sportsmanship in professional sports that we rarely see in politics these days. This country is growing more divided than ever. I could blame numerous people for this, but in a sense, I’d be actively contributing to the problem by doing so, because doing so would just invite defensive reactions from those people and their supporters, and then we’re off to the races. The only winning move, to coin a phrase, is not to play.

I think all of this is at least partly rooted in a misconception about our own nature. We like to think of ourselves as the heroes of our own stories, but heroes aren’t flawed. Thus, we can’t be flawed, right? We’re the hero.

Obviously, it’s not that simple. To fix our flaws, we have to accept them. So we have to accept that we’re flawed. Which means we shouldn’t think of ourselves as heroes in the traditional sense: we’re antiheroes.

The other point is that if we’re heroes, our opponents have to be villains, right? But >that’s an oversimplification too. Some of them might have malicious motives, but many are just ordinary people who are just trying to get by and simply have different beliefs than ours.

Ultimately, outside complete monsters like Hitler and Stalin, I think it’s oversimplifying to call specific people good or bad. People are a mix of both. It can be helpful to identify destructive behaviors and even beliefs, but I think it’s rarely helpful to characterize specific people as good or bad: it ultimately shuts down the rational part of our brains.

Team sports can sometimes engage this sort of tribalistic thinking from its most diehard fans, but I’ve seen just as much if not more of the opposite. Dodgers and Blue Jays fans were, with rare exceptions, extremely respectful to each other and their players during the World Series, and I saw very few cases of Dodgers fans being sore winners after Game 7 – most of them openly commiserated by acknowledging they’d have been devastated if they’d been in the reverse position. Freddie Freeman got a few boos from Atlanta fans, but most received him quite warmly in recognition of his massive contribution to Atlanta’s victory in 2021. Tarik Skubal received a standing ovation when he returned to the Detroit Tigers’ Comerica Park as a Dodger on August 28. And so on.

In short, I wish American politics were more like team sports. If it were, maybe we’d start healing as a country.

On family values

“Family values” has been thrown around so much in contentious political contexts that its original meaning is in danger of being lost to history. I can think of nothing better to represent actual family values than an incredibly wholesome event from this exact game that I only got to see later, since I wasn’t in the best vantage point to see it live. In the ninth inning, when Chris came out to pitch, his second-eldest son was perhaps the first person in the entire stadium to rise to his feet, which indicates two things:

  1. Being able to recognize the significance of that event at his age? That kid knows ball. Not a surprise, since he’s Chris Sale’s son, but still.
  2. Chris probably has no bigger fan on the planet, which indicates that he’s done something right as a parent.

(Also, the announcers didn’t need to tell us he was Chris’ son. We already knew.)

What this tells us: Chris is raising his kids correctly. Which in turn suggests that, in all likelihood, he was raised correctly.
