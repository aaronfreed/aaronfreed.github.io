Āleātōrium semper vincit is Latin for The House Always Wins (or, more literally, The Casino Always Wins). As is often the case, I’ve chosen this album name for rather topical reasons.

As anyone who’s glanced at my website lately has probably noticed, I’m a huge baseball fan. And, as anyone who’s paid even cursory attention to team sports lately has certainly noticed, the cancer of sports betting has become ubiquitous. Hence this album title. I don’t care how much sports knowledge you have – reality is too stochastic to follow predictable patterns. A pitcher can give up two home runs over the course of half a season, then give up two in a single inning. That might be the result of him tipping his pitches, or it might just be the result of baseball being baseball. The sports betting industry would prefer you to believe you can beat the odds. But ultimately, the house always wins.

My previous album, Τὸν ὦνον πάντων καί τὴν ἀξίᾱν οὐδένων (Attic Greek: The Price of Everything and the Value of Nothing), is over two hours long, and I’d put so much work into the Rubicon X soundtrack that afterwards, I took a few months off from writing music. Thus, it felt like an appropriate time to start a new album. Two early tracks on this album were written for Rubicon X at almost the last minute (and thus after most of Τὸν ὦνον πάντων), but the rest of it will be new material.

Track Commentary

  1. “Regressiō ad medium” (8:00) [DR13]

    Latin: “Regression to the Mean”

    A musical representation of recovery from mental disorders that takes an ABA′ form. Originally, each section lasted exactly two minutes, and the A and B sections still do; I faded the A′ section out by the six-minute mark to suggest that the process of recovery is a continuous process. However, the event I wrote this for wound up needing a seamless loop, so I wrote a coda to facilitate this.

    1. «Συμπτώματα» (Attic Greek: “Symptoms”, romanized: «Symptṓmata») (0:00-4:00)

      • The A section, reflecting everyday lived experience prior to the start of therapy.

    2. «Θεραπεία» (Attic Greek: “Therapy”, romanized: «Therapeía») (2:00-4:00)

      • The B section, representing the process of therapy.

    3. «Ἀνάστασις» (Attic Greek: “Anastasis”, romanized: «Anástasis») (4:00-6:00)

      • The A′ section, representing a somewhat (but not fully) recovered experience.

    4. «Αἰωνία περίοδος» (Attic Greek: “Eternal Recurrence”, romanized: «Aiōnía períodos») (6:00-8:00)

      • The coda, signifying recovery’s continuous, sometimes cyclical nature.

    The track also expresses several additional interlinking concepts:

    1. I primarily wrote it in Dorian mode, an entirely symmetrical mode of the diatonic major scale (that is, its ascent and descent use the same intervals in the same order). It’s commonly used both in Gregorian chants and in nature documentaries, as it conveys something of a sense of awe, but its root chord is also minor. This is a deliberate aesthetic choice.
    2. The bass line continuously ascends and descends because not every aspect of recovery is a steady progression. It stays flat for several measures at one point in the B section, reflecting how progress can often feel as if it’s come to a complete halt. It nonetheless resumes its ascent and descent after that.

    3. The use of counterpoint reflects dichotomies between neurodivergent and neurotypical thought and communication. The alternate melody in the A section effectively flips its progession after the first use of counterpoint, only for the response to flip as well.

      Neurodiverse individuals’ nonverbal communication is commonly misread, causing underdiagnosis of mental disorders, problems in the workplace and with relationships, and more. At least part of the issue is that society teaches us to mirror neurotypical body language – an example of masking (concealing our neurodivergence). This, in turn, causes stress, which can turn into its own feedback loop (since the more stressed we are, the worse we are at mirroring), in turn leading to burnout and further complications (including several I already listed at the start of this paragraph).

    4. The A section uses a very simple chord progression, mostly employing variations of A minor, G major, D major, and E major chords. (E major is the only chord in this progression that is non-diatonic to Dorian mode.) The B section deliberately gets more avant-garde and increasingly diverges from the original chord progression, reflecting several ideas:

      1. the ebb and flow of trying to break out of a pattern
      2. how therapies sometimes progress frustratingly slowly, or don’t even seem to be progressing at all
      3. how sometimes the first attempt at a therapy doesn’t even work
    5. The steadily increasing complexity and activity in the melody and harmony and the overall intensity of the arrangement represent how thoughts can become more layered and active as therapy proceeds – a common experience for neurodivergent individuals in particular.
    6. About two-thirds of the way through the B section, most aspects of the arrangement drop out before returning. The idea here is that sometimes it’s necessary to focus on one fundamental idea or behavior rather than try to fix everything at once. This is what ultimately leads to the breakthrough that leads into our A′ section.
    7. The B section also explores the idea of the extinction burst – that is, that a behavior may temporarily increase in frequency after the reward or reinforcement for that behavior is stopped.

    8. The A′ section primarily uses A Mixolydian (which, in terms of mood, effectively lies between A major and A Dorian). It still switches between Mixolydian and Dorian to indicate that, although things have improved, they’re still not perfect.

      The A′ section uses two different Mixolydian chord progressions. The first simply alters the original Dorian progression to be entirely diatonic to Mixolydian. The other applies negative harmony to the original Dorian chords and melody.

      Negative harmony can get complicated, but the Cliffs Notes version is that it flips melodies and chords, turning major chords into minor, minor into major, ascents into descents, and descents into ascents, keeping the same root key if possible⁽¹⁾.

      The negative harmony progression uses variants of A major, B minor, E minor, and D minor, respectively corresponding to the original‘s A minor, G major, D major, and E major. E major is the latter’s only chord that’s non-diatonic to Dorian mode, and Mixolydian is Dorian’s negative harmony counterpart; thus, all of the negative harmony version‘s chords are diatonic to Mixolydian except D minor. I couldn’t decide which Mixolydian progression I preferred, and the two express vastly different moods, so I used both.

    9. At the same time, even in the Dorian sections, the counterpoint melody now generally runs in the same direction, indicating an improvement in overall communication.
    10. The breaks in the melody are deliberate as well, meant to signify that we all need breaks now and then.

    Lastly, some etymological and linguistic trivia:

    • Despite having already titled the piece in Latin, I opted for Greek movement titles due to therapy, symptom, and anastasis (meaning a rebirth or recovery from a debilitating condition) already being Greek.
    • I briefly considered renaming the song «Ὑποστροφή εἰς μέσον» («Hypostrophḗ eís méson», Attic Greek for “Regression to the Mean”) so it’d be titled in the same language as its movements, but it had already been “Regressiō ad medium” for months by then, so I never considered it very seriously.
    • Περίοδος (períodos, used in the coda’s title) is yet another Greek word that English has pilfered for its own nefarious purposes. It’s derived from περί- (perí-, around), and ὁδός (hodós, path or journey), so its literal meaning is return trip or road back.

    • I briefly considered several other names for the coda:

      • „ewige Wiederkunft“, “eternal recurrence” in German (the phrase comes from Friedrich Nietzsche).
      • “Recurrentia aeterna”, “eternal recurrence” in Latin (the origin of both words of the English phrase).
      • «Καιρός ἐστί πλατύς κύκλος» («Kairós estí platýs kýklos»), Attic Greek for “Time is a flat circle” (a True Detective quote summarizing Nietzsche’s concept).

      I ultimately opted for linguistic consistency among movement names, and as much as I love the True Detective quote, it was too inconsistent with the other movement names. That said, I think another True Detective quote sums up this piece’s main theme far more succinctly than I have:

      «Πρίν, ἦν μόνος ὁ ζόφος. Εἰ μοι ἐρωτᾷς, τὸ φῶς ἐστί νῑκῶν.» -Ρυστ Κωλ, Ὀρθός ντετέκτιβ

      «Prín, ên mónos ho zóphos. Ei moi erotás, tò fós estí nīkôn.» –Rust Kōl, Orthós ntetéktib

      “Once, there was only dark. If you ask me, the light‘s winning.” -Rust Cohle, True Detective

  2. «Ῥοδοδάκτυλος Ἠώς» (3:36) [DR12]

    Epic Greek: “Rosy-Fingered Dawn”
    Romanized: «Rhododáktylos Eṓs»

    Inspired by: Mahavishnu Orchestra, “Dawn” (The Inner Mounting Flame, 1971-08-14)

    (John McLaughlin)

    From my Rubicon soundtrack commentary:

    Inspired by Mahavishnu Orchestra’s “Dawn” and named for an epithet that appears throughout Homer’s works. It’s only partly coincidence that, thanks to Christopher Nolan, Homer is in the popular consciousness again, as he’s never far from the popular consciousness. (I kept thinking scenes from Nolan’s film played like dramatic versions of scenes from Monty Python and the Holy Grail until about midway through the film, when I realized I’d gotten it backward: Python had mined Homer for comedic inspiration. The biggest giveaway: the Trojan Rabbit.)

    This one came together extremely quickly; I think it took me about two hours of work. I considered expanding it further, but ultimately I think it works fine the way it is.

  3. «Ἱππῆς ἐπί τῇ αἰγίδι» (6:18) [DR11]

    Attic Greek: “Riders on the Storm”
    Romanized: «Hippês epí tēî aigídi»

    Inspired by: The Doors, “Riders on the Storm” (L.A. Woman, 1971-04-19)

    (John Densmore, Robby Krieger, Ray Manzarek, & Jim Morrison)

    A jazz fusion track heavily inspired by the Doors‘ similarly titled song. I don’t actually like Jim Morrison’s unsettling lyrics for it (not at all helped by his whispered vocals underlying the verses, or the fact that he was dead by the time it came out), but Ray Manzarek’s Fender Rhodes work is absolutely exquisite.

    Much like its namesake, this keeps switching keys, running through a sequence of E Dorian, A melodic major, D Dorian, and G melodic major in the opening 7/8 section, then E Dorian, A Dorian, E Dorian, G major, and E Dorian in the later 5/8 and 6/8 sections (which hew significantly closer to the Doors’ original chord progression). The Doors’ track is already basically jazz fusion, but I played up the jazz influence further (for instance, their track doesn’t use a saxophone).

    This is very much incomplete, hence the fadeout. (I avoid those unless I have a strong artistic reason to use them or intend the track to repeat. Yet currently, this record’s first three tracks fade out – go figure.)

  4. «Λᾱ́ζᾱρος ἐκ μηχανῆς» (4:44) [DR10]

    Attic Greek: “Lazarus from the Machine”
    Romanized: «Lā́zāros ek mēkhanês»

    In case anyone was expecting this entire record to be jazz, I threw this curveball: a drone track composed entirely of nightmarish CS-80 voices. From my Rubicon soundtrack commentary:

    We originally planned on having no music on “Lazarus ex machina”, but we decided it needed music towards the end of development, and since its layout is so convoluted that I couldn’t fade out the music as players fade out the climactic Ascension theme based on their location, I threw together this deliberately minimalistic drone track in about forty minutes (not an exaggeration).

    Utlimately, though, I thought of an approach that would enable us to fade out the music without having to care about the player’s location, so we ultimately reused “Hastur” on this level. I wasn’t really bothered by that, since I wrote this so quickly. For the soundtrack, we still left it in the position of “Lazarus ex machina”, which would otherwise have no track to represent it.

    The familiar Latin phrase Deus ex machina (God from the machine) is calqued from the far less familiar Greek phrase ἀπὸ μεχανῆς θεός (apò mēkhanês theós); as ex is a cognate of ἐκ (ek), I changed it accordingly. I also reordered the words to fit the more familiar Latin order; both orders are valid in both languages. (Languages with noun cases, like Latin and Greek, usually have more flexible word orders than languages without them, like English.)

    The titular Lazarus was a man Jesus resurrected in the New Testament. I’ll spare explaining how this relates to the level, since many people reading this page may not have played Rubicon yet.

    The length is very much deliberate. In societies that use or used writing systems based on Chinese (also including Japan, Korea, and Vietnam), the number four is closely associated with death because the characters 四 (four) and 死 (death) have traditionally been homonyms or near-homonyms (sì and sǐ in Mandarin, shi for both in Japanese, sa for both in Sino-Korean, and tứ and tử in Sino-Vietnamese.) This may have resulted in some languages using other terms for the number four; for instance, in modern Japanese, 四 is more often read as よ (yo) or as よん (yon) than as し (shi).

1.

It’s not possible for Locrian mode, which is an outlier in several ways. It’s the diatonic scale’s sole mode:

  1. That doesn’t transform into another mode of the diatonic scale.
  2. Whose root moves in the transformation (a semitone upward, to be precise).
  3. That doesn’t have a note a perfect fifth above its root note. (This is part of the reason for #1 and #2.)

This is because negative harmony reflects notes across a line halfway between the root and the dominant (i.e., 3½ semitones, or half a perfect fifth, above the root). Thus, on a root of A, we swap the following notes:

  • F↔G♯
  • F♯↔G
  • E↔A
  • D♯↔B♭ or E♭↔A♯
  • D↔B
  • C♯↔C

I didn’t list every note name because a scale containing A can have at most two flats (B♭, E♭) or four sharps (F♯, C♯, G♯, D♯). The reason is relatively straightforward: A♭ would be the next flat, and A♯ the next sharp.

As it happens, though, we still need to list A♯. Why? Two reasons:

  1. A scale can contain both A and E♭, and flats must transform into either sharps or naturals.
  2. Relatedly, A Locrian becomes A♯ Locrian:
    1. E♭→A♯
    2. D→B
    3. C→C♯
    4. B♭→D♯
    5. A→E
    6. G→F♯
    7. F→G♯

    (We could also list it as B♭ Locrian, but we’d have to rename E to F♭ and B to C♭. Gross. I begrudgingly tolerate E♯ and C♭ in six-accidental key signatures, which can‘t otherwise be notated without a flurry of unnecessary accidentals, but seven-accidental key signatures are pretentious garbage.)

The other six modes swap in the following pairs:

  • Phrygian↔Lydian
  • Aeolian↔Ionian
  • Dorian↔Mixolydian

Even though Dorian’s interval composition is symmetrical from the root, we’re reflecting it about a line 3½ semitones above the root, which is why it becomes Mixolydian rather than itself. Walking down the above list of transformations with the A Dorian scale from E gives us A Mixolydian:

  1. E→A
  2. D→B
  3. C→C♯
  4. B→D
  5. A→E
  6. G→F♯
  7. F♯→G

Ultimately, negative harmony pairs each mode with its polar opposite in terms of mood (if it has one):

  • Dorian is the brightest minor mode; Mixolydian is the darkest major mode.
  • In Aeolian, the root, subdominant, and dominant chords are all minor; in Ionian, they’re all major.
  • Phrygian is the darkest minor mode; Lydian is the brighest major mode.
  • Locrian is the only mode with a diminished root chord as its root. As a result, the normal rules don’t apply to it, and applying negative harmony to it just gives us Locrian mode in a different key.
