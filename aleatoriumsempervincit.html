Āleātōrium semper vincit is Latin for The House Always Wins (or, literally, The Casino Always Wins). As is often the case, I’ve chosen a rather topical album title.

If you’ve glanced at my website lately, you probably know I’m a huge baseball fan. And if you’ve paid even cursory attention to sports lately, you certainly know the cancer of sports betting is now ubiquitous. No matter how well you know sports, reality is too stochastic to follow predictable patterns. A pitcher can give up two home runs (or fewer) in a half-season, then two in a single inning.⁽¹⁾ Perhaps he was tipping his pitches, or perhaps, as they say, baseball happens. The sports betting industry wants you to think you can beat the odds. But ultimately, the house always wins.

My previous album, Τὸν ὦνον πάντων καί τὴν ἀξίᾱν οὐδένων ⟨aaronfreed.github.io/tonononpanton.html⟩ (Attic Greek: The Price of Everything and the Value of Nothing), is over two hours long, and I’d put so much work into the Rubicon X soundtrack ⟨aaronfreed.github.io/rubiconxost.html⟩ that afterwards, I took a few months off from writing music. Thus, it felt like an appropriate time to start a new album. Two early tracks on this album were written for Rubicon X at almost the last minute (and thus after most of Τὸν ὦνον πάντων), but the rest of it will be new material.

Track Commentary

  1. “Regressiō ad medium” (8:00) [DR12]

    Latin: “Regression to the Mean”

    A musical representation of recovery from mental disorders that takes an ABA′ form. Originally, this piece had three movements, each lasting exactly two minutes, with the third fading out by the six-minute mark to suggest that the process of recovery never ends. However, the event I wrote this for needed it to loop seamlessly, so I wrote a coda to facilitate this, resulting in four movements, all titled in Attic Greek:

    1. «Συμπτώματα» (“Symptoms”, «Symp­tṓ­ma­ta») (0:00-2:00) [DR11]

      • The A section, reflecting everyday lived experience prior to the start of therapy.

    2. «Θεραπεία» (“Therapy”, «The­ra­pe­ía») (2:00-4:00) [DR11]

      • The B section, representing the process of therapy.

    3. «Ἀνάστασις» (“Anastasis”, «Aná­sta­sis») (4:00-6:00) [DR11]

      • The A′ section, representing a somewhat (but not fully) recovered experience.

    4. «Αἰωνία παλιγγενεσία» (“Eter­nal Re­cur­rence”, «Aiōnía pa­lin­ge­ne­sía») (6:00-8:00) [DR11]

      • The coda, signifying recovery’s continuous, sometimes cyclical nature.

    The shifts between movements α, β, and γ are extremely obvious, but the shift between movement γ and δ is more a gradual transition than a sharp dichotomy. Make of that what you will. (I find it especially fitting that ἀνάστασις and παλιγγενεσία are at least plesionyms if not synonyms.)

    (The “alternate mixes” folder features a variant specifically made for looping. I also boosted the gain in the quiet parts, since the album mix’s loudness range of over 15 LU risks either the quiet parts being inaudible or the loud ones drowning out people’s conversations. The loop variant tones down the dynamic shifts substantially without eliminating them entirely, resulting in a loudness range of around 6 LU.)

    This track also expresses several additional interlinking concepts:

    1. I primarily wrote it in Dorian mode, an entirely symmetrical mode of the diatonic major scale (that is, its ascent and descent use the same intervals in the same order). It’s often used both in Gregorian chants and in nature documentaries, as it conveys a sense of awe and hope, but its root chord is also minor. The general mood conveyed here is that things aren’t great now, but there’s a chance they’ll improve.

      This is a deliberate aesthetic choice. A person must be receptive to therapy for it to be helpful. In short, if you refuse to accept that you need help, you won’t get better. By corollary, if you refuse to accept your own imperfections, you can’t hope to improve them. If you go to therapy believing it can work, it has a much greater chance of success. If you have to be dragged kicking and screaming, you might as well have stayed home. It’s a weirdly self-fulfilling prophecy.

      In short, I used Dorian mode because it reflects the necessary mindset for successful therapy.

    2. The bass line continuously ascends and descends, just as recovery is rarely a steady progression. It plateaus for several measures in one part of «Θεραπεία», reflecting how progress often feels like it’s come to a complete halt, before nonetheless resuming its ascent and descent.

    3. I used counterpoint to reflect dualities in neurodiverse and neurotypical communication and thought. The alternate melody in «Συμπτώματα» effectively flips its progession after the first use of counterpoint, only for the response to flip as well.

      Neurodiverse individuals’ nonverbal communication is often misread, causing underdiagnosis of mental disorders, problems in the workplace and with relationships, and more. Experience teaches us to mirror neurotypical body language (a type of masking, or concealing neurodivergent traits) if we wish to be understood. This stresses us out, often creating its own feedback loop (the more stressed we are, the worse we are at masking), in turn causing burnout and further complications (including several I already listed at the start of this paragraph).

    4. «Συμπτώματα» uses a very simple chord progression, mostly based on variants of A minor, G major, D major, and E major. (E major is this progression’s only chord that’s non-diatonic to Dorian mode.) «Θεραπεία» deliberately gets more avant-garde and increasingly diverges from the original chord progression, reflecting several ideas:

      1. the ebb and flow of trying to break out of a pattern
      2. how therapy can progress frustratingly slowly, or not even seem to be progressing at all
      3. how sometimes the first attempt at a therapy doesn’t even work
    5. The steadily increasing complexity and activity in the melody and harmony and the overall intensity of the arrangement represent how thoughts can become more layered and active as therapy proceeds – a common experience for neurodivergent individuals in particular.
    6. Just before the halfway mark in «Θεραπεία», most aspects of the arrangement drop out, then return. The idea here is that sometimes one must focus on one fundamental idea or behavior rather than try to fix everything at once. This is what ultimately leads to the breakthrough that leads into «Ἀνάστασις».
    7. «Θεραπεία» also explores the idea of the extinction burst – that is, a temporary increase in a behavior’s frequency after rewards or reinforcements for that behavior end.

    8. «Ἀνάστασις» largely uses A Mixolydian (A major and A Dorian’s effective midpoint in mood – and on the circle of fifths ⟨aaronfreed.github.io/musicalmodes.html⟩). Mixolydian, the diatonic scale’s darkest major mode, expresses a more wistful, nostalgic, or bittersweet mood than Ionian (standard major) due partly to its minor fifth (dominant) chord. We still periodically revert to Dorian to indicate that, while things have improved, progress requires constant effort. I also used two near-opposite methods to adapt the Dorian melodies and chords to Mixolydian:

      A minor, D major, E major, G major

      1. Merely by altering them to fit the A Mixolydian scale:

        • A major, D major, E minor, G major
      2. By inverting them using negative harmony (explained below):
        • A major, E minor, D minor, B minor

        (I’ve kept chords in the same places across transformations; italicized chords that are non-diatonic to their respective modes; and printed major chords in bold small caps. I’ve also ignored this song’s many seventh and ninth chords to keep this explanation simple.)

      Negative harmony (to oversimplify) flips melodies and chords, turning major chords minor, minor chords major, ascents into descents, and descents into ascents, ideally with the same root⁽²⁾. Mixolydian is Dorian’s negative harmony counterpart; thus, as seen above, the negative harmony chords are all diatonic to A Mixolydian except D minor – the negative harmony counterpart of E major, the original progression’s only chord that’s non-diatonic to A Dorian.

      I liked both Mixolydian progressions well enough, and the two express different enough moods that it felt worthwhile to use both. So I did.

    9. Even in the Dorian sections of «Ἀνάστασις», the counterpoint melody now generally runs in the same direction, indicating an improvement in overall communication.
    10. The several breaks in the melody are also deliberate. We all need breaks now and then.

    Lastly, some etymological and linguistic trivia:

    • Despite this piece’s Latin title, I named its movements in Greek because therapy, symptom, and anastasis (i.e., a rebirth or recovery from a debilitating condition) were already Greek.
    • I briefly considered renaming the song «Ὑποστροφή εἰς μέσον» («Hypostrophḗ eís méson», Attic Greek for “Regression to the Mean”) so it’d be titled in the same language as its movements, but it had already been “Regressiō ad medium” for months by then, so I never considered it very seriously.
    • The coda’s title can also mean “Eternal Rebirth”. More specifically, palingenesis (literally, again-birth) can mean a spiritual rebirth, especially through reincarnation. However, I had a different sense of palingenesis in mind that Arthur Schopenhauer would’ve appreciated: history’s infinitely cyclical nature. (History’s motto, Schopenhauer wrote, should be “Eādem, sed aliter”, a Latin phrase meaning roughly likewise, but otherwise or the same, but different.)

    • I originally called the coda «Αἰωνία περίοδος» (romanized: «Aiōnía períodos»). Περίοδος, yet another word English has pilfered for its own nefarious purposes, is derived from περί- (perí-, around), and ὁδός (hodós, path or journey), so it literally means return trip or road around. Needless to say, I didn’t change the meaning much, but παλιγγενεσία’s specific connotation of historical cycles was too good to pass up.

    • I considered three other names for the coda. (The first two also mean “eternal recurrence”.)

      1. „ewige Wiederkunft“, Friedrich Nietzsche’s original German phrase
      2. “Recurrentia aeterna”, the Latin etyma of both words of the English translation
      3. «Καιρός ἐστί πλατύς κύκλος» («Kairós estí platýs kýklos»), Attic Greek for “Time is a flat circle” (a True Detective quote summarizing Nietzsche’s concept)

      I ultimately opted for linguistic consistency among movement names, and as much as I love the True Detective quote, it was too long to feel consistent with the other movement names. That said, I think another True Detective quote sums up this piece’s main theme far more succinctly than I have, and for some reason, I felt compelled to translate it to Greek:

      «Πρίν, ἦν μόνος ζόφος. Ἐᾱ́ν μοι ἐρωτᾷς, τό φῶς ἐστί νῑκῶν.» -Ρυστ Κωλ, Ὀρθός ντετέκτιβ

      «Prín, ên mónos zóphos. Eā́n moi erōtaîs, tó fôs estí nīkôn.» –Rust Kōl, Orthós ntetéktib

      “Once, there was only dark. If you ask me, the light‘s winning.” -Rust Cohle, True Detective

      Maybe, in a way, that conflicts with this album’s title. Maybe, every now and then, you can beat the odds.

      Just don’t bet your life savings on it.

  2. «Ῥοδοδάκτυλος Ἠώς» (3:36) [DR12]

    Epic Greek: “Rosy-Fingered Dawn”
    Romanized: «Rhododáktylos Eṓs»

    Inspired by: Mahavishnu Orchestra, “Dawn” (The Inner Mounting Flame, 1971-08-14)

    (John McLaughlin)

    From my Rubicon soundtrack commentary:

    Inspired by Mahavishnu Orchestra’s “Dawn” and named for an epithet that appears throughout Homer’s works. It’s only partly coincidence that, thanks to Christopher Nolan, Homer is in the popular consciousness again, as he’s never far from the popular consciousness. (I kept thinking scenes from Nolan’s film played like dramatic versions of scenes from Monty Python and the Holy Grail until about midway through the film, when I realized I’d gotten it backward: Python had mined Homer for comedic inspiration. The biggest giveaway: the Trojan Rabbit.)

    This one came together extremely quickly; I think it took me about two hours of work. I considered expanding it further, but ultimately I think it works fine the way it is.

  3. «Ή αἰγίς τῆς Ἱππολύτης» (6:56) [DR11]

    Attic Greek: “Hippolyta’s Aegis”
    Romanized: «He aigís tês Hippolýtēs»

    Inspired by: The Doors, “Riders on the Storm” (L.A. Woman, 1971-04-19)

    (John Densmore, Robby Krieger, Ray Manzarek, & Jim Morrison)

    A jazz fusion track heavily inspired by the Doors‘ “Riders on the Storm”. I don’t actually like Jim Morrison’s unsettling lyrics for it (not at all helped by his whispered vocals underlying the verses, or the fact that he was dead by the time it came out), but Ray Manzarek’s Fender Rhodes work is absolutely exquisite – in particular, it imitates raindrops falling in a simple but brilliant way; what it’s meant to represent will be immediately obvious to listeners.

    Much like its namesake, this keeps switching keys, running through a sequence of E Dorian, A melodic major, D Dorian, and G melodic major in the opening 𝟕
    𝟖     section, then E Dorian, A Dorian, E Dorian, G major, and E Dorian in the later 𝟓
    𝟖     and 𝟔
    𝟖     sections (which hew significantly closer to the Doors’ original chord progression). The Doors’ track is already basically jazz fusion, but I played up the jazz influence further (for instance, their track doesn’t use a saxophone).

    This is still incomplete, hence the fadeout. (I avoid those unless I have a strong artistic reason to use them or intend a track to repeat. This record’s first three tracks still fade out – go figure.)

    To answer the inevitable question about why the first three tracks on this album are jazz, it’s mostly because Logic Pro recently upgraded its saxophone voice. Both Logic’s previous sax voices and, uncharacteristically, ComposerCloud+’s sax voices are extremely basic and lacking in articulations. Logic’s new sax voices are exquisite, and that’s not just me saying that – one of my friends, himself a saxophone player who attended Berklee College of Music, agrees.

    The song title is a bilingual pun. The Attic Greek for “Riders on the Storm” is «Ἱππῆς ἐπί τῇ αἰγίδι» («Hippês epí teî aigídi»), my original name for this song. I renamed it because αἰγίς (aigís) refers to both a storm and a shield, and ἵππος (híppos, horse) is the root of both ἱππεύς (hippeús, rider) and Ἱππολύτη (Hippolýtē, literally horse releaser), whom I find to be a fascinating mythological figure.

  4. «Λᾱ́ζᾱρος ἐκ μηχανῆς» (4:44) [DR10]

    Attic Greek: “Lazarus from the Machine”
    Romanized: «Lā́zāros ek mēkhanês»

    In case anyone was expecting this entire record to be jazz, I threw this curveball: a drone track composed entirely of nightmarish CS-80 voices. From my Rubicon soundtrack commentary:

    We planned for “Lazarus ex machina” to have no music until near the end of development. Its layout is so convoluted that I couldn’t use players’ location as a cue to fade out the music as they approach the climactic Ascension theme, so I threw together this deliberately minimalistic drone track in about forty minutes (not an exaggeration).

    Ultimately, I thought of an approach that enabled us to fade out the music without referring to the player’s location, so we instead reused “Hastur” on this level. I wasn’t really bothered by that, since I wrote this so quickly. For the soundtrack, we still left it in the position of “Lazarus ex machina”, which would otherwise have no track to represent it.

    The familiar Latin phrase Deus ex machina (God from the machine) is calqued from the far less familiar Greek phrase ἀπὸ μεχανῆς θεός (apò mēkhanês theós); as ex is a cognate of ἐκ (ek), I changed it accordingly. I also reordered the words to fit the more familiar Latin order; both orders are valid in both languages. (Languages with noun cases, like Latin and Greek, usually have more flexible word orders than languages without them, like English.)

    The titular Lazarus was a man Jesus resurrected in the New Testament. Many people reading this may not have played Rubicon yet, so I won’t explain the story significance beyond that.

    The length is very much deliberate. In societies that use or used writing systems based on Chinese (also including Japan, Korea, and Vietnam), the number four is closely associated with death because the characters 四 (four) and 死 (death) have traditionally been homonyms or near-homonyms (sì and sǐ in Mandarin, shi for both in Japanese, sa for both in Sino-Korean, and tứ and tử in Sino-Vietnamese.) This may have resulted in some languages using other terms for the number four; for instance, in modern Japanese, 四 is more often read as よ (yo) or as よん (yon) than as し (shi).

  5. ｢ユニコーンの折り紙｣ (3:30) [DR10]

    Japanese: “Origami Unicorn”
    Rōmaji: ｢Yunikōn no origami｣

    A Blade Runner-inspired ambient piece whose only instrumentation, thus far, is CS-80 chords. Its first half consists of a drone on C♯ major and variants thereof (e.g., C♯7add ♯11, C♯add ♭6, and C♯maj7).

    Its second half, while keeping the C♯ pedal tone that extends throughout the entire song, also adds a series of chords descending the C♯ Phrygian scale. Most of these chords are major, apart from the G♯ and F♯ chords, which produces a very uncanny effect. As this chord progression repeats a few times, I also add a series of ascending bass notes. These notes vary somewhat between repetitions, although notably, each of the last two outlines a G♯ diminished seventh chord in its second half.

    All of this combines to produce a very moody, atmospheric, spacy-sounding track. Several of these chords (particularly the add ♭6 chord) are ubiquitous in space-related soundtracks for a reason. This remains a very early work in progress, but it feels like it’s going somewhere.

    The song title refers to the origami figures Edward James Olmos’ character Gaff is fond of folding. In one memorable scene of the film, he gives one to Harrison Ford’s character Rick Deckard. (I was tempted to name this track ｢大谷翔平の折り紙｣, i.e., ｢Ōtani Shohei no origami｣, but the title is already obscure enough without me adding another layer of obscurity atop that.)

A few notes on the above tables:

  • The columns list the root of the scale being transformed; the rows list the transformed note. At their intersection lies that note’s negative harmony counterpart for that root.
  • The red notes are the transformations for the note a perfect fifth above the scale root (usually the fifth note of the scale, except in Locrian mode).
  • The blue notes are the scale roots’ transformations. Blue and red notes swap places.
  • The red and blue borders represent notes outside the scale’s standard perfect fifth.
  • The red and blue color-coding refers to the Doppler effect.
  • The green border represents the axis of reflection for a given root.

  • If you’re not in Locrian mode and your key signature has sharps or flats, you may need to switch to the other accidental type to avoid awful key signatures. (You may also need to transform a root to its enharmonic equivalent, e.g., G♭ to F♯.)

    What do I mean by awful key signatures? Glad you asked.

    G♭ Lydian (G♭ A♭ B♭ C D♭ E♭ F) turns into F♯ Phrygian (F♯ G A B C♯ D E) because G♭ Phrygian is G♭ A𝄫 B𝄫 C♭ D♭ E𝄫 F♭. For those in the cheap seats, we just replaced two sharps with four flats and three double-flats. Ain’t nobody got time for that.

    (At least 𝄫 has a clear meaning. 𝄪, the double-sharp, is an abomination unto Nuggan.)

    Lydian (+5×♭) and Phrygian (+5×♯) are likeliest to experience this problem, followed by Ionian (+3×♭) and Ionian (+3×♯). It rarely affects Mixolydian (+♭) or Dorian (+♯). Locrian, meanwhile, changes its root, so key signature nonsense is the least of our worries there.

Note that a scale containing A can have at most two flats (B♭, E♭) or four sharps (F♯, C♯, G♯, D♯). The reason is relatively straightforward: A♭ would be the next flat, and A♯ the next sharp. No mode of the diatonic major scale containing either of those notes can also contain A♮. (It can, however, contain G♯ and A, or A and B♭, although not all three.)

As it happens, though, A♯ still pertains to a transformation involving a scale rooted on A. Why? Two reasons:

  1. A Locrian contains both A and E♭. Flats should transform into either sharps or naturals.
  2. Relatedly, A Locrian becomes A♯ Locrian. (Originals on left, transformations on right.)

A few other notes on the above table:

  • The notes shift places between pre- and post-transformation scales. I list the original scale in descending order, starting on the fifth scale degree; this results in the transformation appearing in the more familiar ascending order, starting on the root.
  • Besides Locrian, each mode and its mirror image in mood (sorry, not sorry) are paired.
    • Dorian is the brightest minor mode; Mixolydian is the darkest major mode.
    • Aeolian’s root, subdominant, and dominant chords are all minor; Ionian’s are major.
    • Phrygian is the darkest minor mode; Lydian is the brighest major mode.
    • Locrian is the only mode with a diminished root chord, so the normal rules don’t apply to it, and applying negative harmony to it just gives us Locrian in a different key.
  • Dorian’s interval composition is symmetrical from the root, but it becomes Mixolydian rather than itself because we’re reflecting it on a line 3½ semitones above the root.
