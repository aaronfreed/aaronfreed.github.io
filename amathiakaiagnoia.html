Συνοπτική ἠθική ἀνάλυσις τῆς ἀμαθίᾱς καί ἀγνοίᾱς

Synoptikḗ ēthikḗ análysis tês amathíās kaí agnoíās

A Brief Ethical Analysis of Ignorance

by Aaron Freed ⟨aaronfreed.github.io/aboutme.html⟩

An important Socrates quote is sometimes translated along the lines: There is only one good, wisdom, and one evil, ignorance. That may sound absurd, but it’s a woefully inadequate translation: Socrates is describing concepts that are elusive in English. So let’s examine his original words:

Attic Greek: «Ἕν μόνον ἀγαθόν εἶναι, τὴν ἐπιστήμην, καί ἓν μόνον κακόν, τὴν ἀμαθίᾱν.»
Romanized: «Hén mónon agathón eînai, tḕn epistḗmēn, kaí hén mónon kakón, tḕn amathíān.»
Literally: There is only one good, learning, and there is only one evil, not learning.
Figuratively: Learning is the only good, and refusal to learn the only evil.

A few Greek words are crucial to understanding this quote:

  • The word ἐπιστήμη⁽¹⁾ can mean science, knowledge, or wisdom. Encapsulating these all as learning not only seems fair to me but, I think, strengthens Socrates’ point.
  • Translating ἀμαθίᾱ⁽²⁾ as ignorance oversimplifies its full meaning: wilful ignorance. It’s derived from words for not learning, which is thus my literal translation of it.
    • The Ancient Greeks contrasted this with ἄγνοια,⁽³⁾ or not knowing. This is “innocent” ignorance: we’re all born knowing nothing, and not knowing is curable in those who want to learn.
    • To put it another way: “You can lead a horse to knowledge, but you can’t make it think.”⁽⁴⁾

Socrates is not condemning ἄγνοια, only ἀμαθίᾱ, a distinction too easily lost in English. I thus modestly propose adding amathia, amathic, agnoia, and agnoic to our language. The word ignorance holds ethical connotations that the agnoic don’t deserve, and it fails to diagnose the source of the problem for either amathia or agnoia. The Greek terms carry neither limitation. Sloppy language encourages sloppy thinking, and we can’t afford to be sloppy in addressing problems as severe as amathia. The discrepancy is too important.

I considered the idea that they’d used German declensions, but Schema ⟨en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Schema#German⟩ pluralizes to Schemata, Schemas, and Schemen in all four German cases. (Schemata is the truest to its etymon, σχῆμα [romanized: skhêma; Λογεῖον ⟨logeion.uchicago.edu/σχῆμα⟩, LSJ ⟨lsj.gr/wiki/σχῆμα⟩, Wiktionary ⟨en.wiktionary.org/wiki/σχῆμα⟩], whose nominative plural is σχήματα [romanized: skhḗmata].)

Another possibility is that this is an obscure reference to Egyptian mythology, whose deities can be romanized in several different ways. The deity most frequently romanized as Sekhmet could, theoretically, also be romanized as Scheme. The possessive form of Scheme in German is simply Schemes, so „Schemes Amathia“ would thus mean “Sekhmet’s Willful Ignorance”. I consider this equally implausible.

At any rate, it’s certainly not Modern Greek: the modern spelling is αμάθεια (amátheia).
