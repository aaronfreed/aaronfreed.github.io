Introduction

Αἱ βραχῑ́ους ἄκραι is Attic Greek for The Shorter Peaks⁽¹⁾ (romanized: Hai brakhī́ous ákrai). It’s a companion to Τέτταρες πλευραί, ὕμνοι, καιροί, καί ἄνεμοι (Attic Greek: Four Sides, Songs, Seasons, & Winds, romanized: Téttares pleuraí, hýmnoi, kairoí, kaí ánemoi), an anthology of four twenty-minute pieces from 2024 to 2026 that I compiled last month. This collection spans a significantly longer time, from 2014 to 2026, and its eighteen tracks range from four and a half to fourteen minutes in length, for a mean of 8:48 per track. This is roughly typical of my output (Τέτταρες πλευραί was a deliberate sampling of outliers).

Why, though?

Why did you duplicate so many tracks from Selected Works, 2023-2024, and Iānuāriō Aprīlem MMXXV?

I wanted to re-anthologize that period of my output for several reasons, among them:

  1. Those two releases were collectively four hours long – roughly the same length as this and Τέτταρες πλευραί, which collectively cover a much longer span of material.
  2. I compiled Selected Works, 2023-2024 over eighteen months ago and Iānuāriō Aprīlem MMXXV over a year ago. Parts of the former now bring up unpleasant memories (no, I won’t elaborate further). Re-anthologizing that period gives me a collection I can listen to from start to finish with no such issues.
  3. It pleases me that, because Τέτταρες πλευραί is an anthology of my four longest works, new listeners now may start with a shorter anthology of my longer tracks or a longer anthology of my shorter tracks.
  4. I regretted some of my omissions from Selected Works, 2023-2024. (Granted, one of them fell outside the eponymous timespan.) Anthologizing those tracks alongside my work from the past year would’ve felt incongruous – while this set also takes a non-chronological approach, it at least front-loads most of its older material and backloads most of its newer material, and it’s a less jarring approach than jumping exclusively between 2014 material, 2022–2023 material, and 2025–2026 material would’ve been.

Why isn’t this a chronological anthology?

Again, several reasons:

  1. Define “chronological”. The versions of “Swirls” here are some of my older work. “𓈖𓐍𓅱𓏏𓀁 𓈖𓏏 𓉡𓁥” is one of the newest tracks here. Should the two versions of “Swirls / 𓈖𓐍𓅱𓏏𓀁 𓈖𓏏 𓉡𓁥” go near the start, go near the end, or be split up? A non-chronological approach sidesteps this question entirely.
  2. A chronological approach would’ve required multiple versions of some songs to appear in direct succession. I find that far less interesting than putting them near the start and end of a set.

  3. I value physical media, so I usually tailor my releases to fit on either one or two audio CDs, and while my music will probably never be released on vinyl, I try to arrange songs on my albums so that they could be released on vinyl with no significant cuts or alterations to track order. I enjoy old vinyl records’ pacing and try to duplicate that with my digital output as much as I can. This set is no exception:

    Side A (19:47): 1.1 & 1.2

    Side B (19:46): 1.3 & 1.4

    Side C (20:46): 1.5 & 1.6

    Side D (19:00): 1.7 & 1.8

    Side E (19:20): 2.1 & 2.2

    Side F (20:42): 2.3, 2.4, & 2.5

    Side G (18:58): 2.6, 2.7, & 2.8

    Side H (20:00): 2.9 & 2.10

    Vinyl starts losing fidelity when sides creep too far above twenty minutes in length, and keeping side lengths similar ensures greater consistency in sound quality across the whole album. The above sides have a mean length of 19:47⅜, a median length of 19:46½, a range of 1:48 (roughly 9.10% of either average), a standard deviation of roughly 38¾ seconds (roughly 3.25% of either average), and no outliers.

    I probably could’ve arranged these tracks chronologically in a way that would fit on physical media, but:

    1. I’d likely have had to make significant alterations to the material to do so, and I wouldn’t have considered that an especially honest presentation.

    2. The odds that I wouldn’t have had to make such alterations were mathematically small enough that I didn’t even consider it worth investigating whether it was possible.

    (I shudder to think what I’ll do if I ever write a thirty-minute piece. Forty-minute pieces are easy to handle: just bake in a convenient split point, like the one Jethro Tull wrote into Thick as a Brick. But thirty minutes? Clearly, the best solution is to write another ten. …I’m not even sure I’m being facetious here.)

  4. “Supper’s Ready (excerpt) / Love” would be the first piece chronologically. If I didn’t love it, it wouldn’t be here, but it’s an insufficiently attention-grabbing intro – it’s just a solo piano performance, and while I’m a competent pianist, I’m hardly Yuja Wang or Rick Wakeman; I’m more like Tom Lehrer (RIP) without the sense of humor. (Lehrer often quoted a review saying that he “plays the piano acceptably”.)

Why are so few of your original songs’ titles in English?

I struggle to come up with original titles, so I often just translate the name of a piece’s biggest inspiration to another language. Moreover, most of my English titles are meaningless:

  • “Blood on the Sequencer” and “The Pipeline, Filling In Time” were literally the only phrases I could think of when I saved them, and I never thought of better names for either. (The latter comes from the lyrics to Pink Floyd’s “Welcome to the Machine”, but that doesn’t clarify anything about my song because it doesn’t really bear any stylistic resemblance to its eponym.)
  • “Disco Apocalypse in 𝟓
    𝟒     (co-starring the delicious talents of Logic Pro)    ”     is just a riff on Genesis’ “Apocalypse in 𝟗
    𝟖     (co-starring the delicious talents of Gabble Ratchet)    ”     (whose intro it quotes directly) and several disco song titles (e.g., The Trammps’ “Disco Inferno”, David Shire’s “Night on Disco Mountain”).

The sole exception to the “my English titles are meaningless” rule is, perhaps unsurprisingly, “Love”.

But why are so many of my titles that aren’t directly stolen borrowed from other works in foreign languages? I often find them more fitting for a given context than English:

  1. I may be employing either thematic resonance or some form of irony:
    1. Hathor was an Egyptian goddess. I titled a piece bearing her name in Middle Egyptian.
    2. The ancient Greek Cynics were the antithesis of their name’s modern definition, so I translated a famous Oscar Wilde quote (“What is a cynic? A man who knows the price of everything and the value of nothing”) to Attic Greek («Τίς ἐστί Κυνικός? Ἀνήρ ὅς γιγνώσκει τὸν ὦνον πάντων καί τὴν ἀξίᾱν οὐδένων»).
  2. I may be expressing an ambiguity that’s inexpressible in English:
    1. Aequilibrium harmoniae is Latin for both The Balance of Harmony and The Harmony of Balance.
    2. Mūsica ex tempore malōrum is Latin for both Music from a Time of Disasters and Music from After the Opportunity of the Disasters.
    3. Κατηγορική ἀπολογίᾱ is Greek for Categorical Apology, Accusatory Apologia, and several other meanings, to the extent of arguably being untranslatably ambiguous; also, κατηγορίᾱ and ἀπολογίᾱ are antonyms.
  3. I may have aesthetic or artistic reasons:
    1. Ancient languages often trigger emotions in us that English doesn’t and quite possibly can’t.
    2. I may simply prefer how the title sounds in a foreign language.

    # Note
    1. In the sense of duration, not height (though βραχύς can mean either; it is a cognate of Latin brevis and thus our own brevity, breve, and brief, and it is also linguistically related to the English word brachiosaur by way of βραχίων, meaning the shorter one and referring to the upper arm. Ἄκρον and ἄκρα, meanwhile, are linguistically related to acrostic, acropolis, acronym, acrobat, acrophobia, and Akron). Other valid translations of this phrase include Αἱ βραχῑ́ονες ἄκραι, Τά βραχῑ́ω ἄκρα, and Τά βραχῑ́ονα ἄκρα.
