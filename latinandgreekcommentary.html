Eternal X

Prologus: Fātum
(Prologue: Destiny)

To my disappointment, this is the only one of the chapter titles that didn’t have an exact cognate in Latin with an identical meaning. Destiny is actually the noun form of the verb destine, meaning the state of being destined or the act of destining; destine in turn is derived from the Latin verb dēstinō. It displaced the Old English word wyrd, which was in turn an ancestor of (and was pronounced identically to) Modern English weird. Dēstinō is also etymologically related to dēstinātiō, whence destination.

Unfortunately, there is no suitable noun form of dēstinō that means destiny, so I went with fātum instead. Fātum has its own interesting etymology, but that’s another story.

Lātus procul nūllius locī

Title

My non-literal Latin translation of “The Far Side of Nowhere”. It literally means “The Far Side of No Place”, but in Latin phrases like this, it’s best to use the genitive case, which nusquam (nowhere), being an adverb, does not have. “Lātus procul dē nusquam” would be a literal (and more awkward) translation.

Caput ūnum: Memoria
(Chapter I: Memory)

Deja Vu All Over Again

Map Writing

Erāmus omnēs hūc ante.
(RIP DC, MCMXLI–MMXXIII)

Translates as “We have all been here before,” a quote from Crosby, Stills, & Nash’s “Déjà vu” (Déjà vu, 1970-03-11). “DC” is, of course, David Crosby, who lived from 1941 to 2023.

Σηπτοκοκκική Φφορυγγίτιδα
(Septococcal Pfhoryngitis)

Title

The map name is a pun on the phrase streptococcal pharyngitis, or strep throat. Streptococcal is derived from στρεπτός (streptós, meaning twisted), and κόκκος (kókkos, meaning seed). Pharyngitis is derived from φᾰ́ρῠγξ (phắrŭnx, meaning throat) and -ῖτις (-îtis), which denoted a connection to a place in ancient Greek, but has become -ίτιδα (-ítida) in modern Greek, denoting inflammations. There’s an additional pun here on σηπτικός (>septikós, whence septic), meaning characterized by putridity.

Map Writing

Σηπτοκοκκική Φφορυγγίτιδα
Ἐκτῐσμένη ύπο
Δόν-Μάρτιν Αντέλλ
Ἀαρών Φρέεδ

A crude transliteration of the map title into Greek, from which the level’s entire title is directly derived (see directly above). An exact Romanization would be:

Septokokkiké Phforungítida
Ektĭsménē úpo
Dón-Mártin Antéll
Aarṓn Fréed

Originally, I erroneously used Κτιζομένη (Ktizoménē), the passive present participle of κτίζω (ktízō, to build). I also took an artistic license in using Φφ as a rough equivalent of pfh – Bungie occasionally misspelled it as Phfor anyway (in fact, this may have been the original spelling), so I don’t mind. (The Romans would have insisted on romanizing this as Phph.)

Δυσμεντρία
(Dysmentria)

Title

“Dysmentria” is a pun on two Ancient Greek words:

  • Δυσμετρία (dusmetría): dysmetria, meaning an inability to control or limit muscular movements;
  • Μένος (ménos): mind, desire, anger, violence, etc.

Thus, it might be interpreted as meaning an inability to limit or control one’s mind, desire, anger, or violence.

An acutal ancient Greek word related to this concept is ᾰ̓κρᾰ́τειᾰ (romanized ăkrắteiă) or ᾰ̓κρᾰσῐ́ᾱ (romanized ăkrăsĭ́ā), which exists in modern English under the spellings akrasia and acrasia, which means a lack of physical or (especially) mental strength, poor willpower, and a tendency to act against one’s better judgement.

Sakhmet Rising

Title

A reference to Egyptian mythology. Sakhmet (also seen spelled as Sekhmet, Sachmis, Scheme, and others) is an aspect of Hathor (or, in some traditions, Bast) that emerges when she is sufficiently angered. Leela later writes (in “Bug-Eyed in Space” and “Once More Unto the Breach”):

Sakhmet served as protector of the pharaohs and led them in warfare, was called upon to ward off disease, subdued Ra’s enemies as the Eye of Ra, and was known as “One Before Whom Evil Trembles”. But she also had many much darker aspects, as suggested by her other titles, “Mistress of Dread”, “Lady of Slaughter”, and “She Who Mauls”.

The gods once unleased Sakhmet to punish humanity, but quickly regretted it, as her rampage far exceeded their intentions; her bloodthirst (which is not a metaphor) grew with each person she slaughtered, until she threatened to wipe out humanity entirely. Her rampage was only placated when Ra filled a lake with beer he’d dyed red to resemble blood. She drank all of it, which got her so drunk she gave up the slaughter and returned peacefully to being Hathor.

Players are invited to draw their own conclusions.

Map Writing

“Nōnne potes mē pulsantēm audīre?”
In memoriam C. Watts (MCMXLI–MMXXI)

A tribute to Rolling Stones drummer Charlie Watts, who passed away in 2021; I was working on this map when I found out and just wrote a tribute. The first line literally means “Are you not able to hear my knocking?”, a reference to “Can’t You Hear Me Knocking?”, a Sticky Fingers (1971-04-23) track with a stunning spontaneous jam that erupted after guitarist Mick Taylor simply kept playing after the song ended. Watts, bassist Bill Wyman, and conga player Rocky Dijon lay down an absolute monster of a groove throughout the entire track, which is easily my favorite Stones song.

(I do feel at least Taylor should be credited as a cowriter along Mick Jagger and Keith Richards. Cases could also be made for everyone who contributed to the jam: Watts, Wyman, Dijon, saxophonist Bobby Keys, organist Billy Preston, and percussionist/producer Jimmy Miller.)

Remedial Chaos Theory

Title

Named for a classic Community episode (S3E4, 2011-10-13). Although Community mostly plays this for laughs, its take on multiverse theory is very similar to Eternal’s: even slight differences in outcome create new timelines. This episode felt like a perfect reference for one of the levels following Eternal’s first timeline branch.

Map Writing

Nōn obligās habilitāre lūcem rubeam.

Literally something like “You’re not obligated to enable the red light”. Figuratively, “You don’t have to put on the red light.” In the Community episode “Remedial Chaos Theory” (S3E4, 2011-10-13), the Police’s “Roxanne” (single, 1978-04-07; Outlandos d’Amour, 1978-11-17) plays something like seven times, usually roughly up to this line.

Roots and Radicals

Title

A radical is the symbol used to represent a mathematical root: √4, for instance, refers to the square root of 4, while ³√8 refers to the cubic root of 8. (These both have the same answer, namely 2.)

Ūniversum tangēns
(The Tangent Universe)

Title

As with all of the Inti Station levels, this one derives its title from Donnie Darko (2001-01-19, dir. Richard Kelly) or supplemental material thereof.

Map Writing

Incipimur id dēnuō.
Cūr nōn possumus incipere id dēnuō?
Modo sine nōs incipere id dēnuō,
Et erimus bonī.
Hoc tempus faciēmus id rēctē.
Est opportūnitās ultima nostra ignōscere nōs ipsōs.
–MB, MMIX

This translates roughly as:

Let’s start it over again.
Why can’t we start it over again?
Just let us start it over again,
And we’ll be good.
This time we’ll get it right.
It’s our last chance to forgive ourselves.

Which is a quote from Muse’s “Exogenesis: Symphony Part III (Redemption)” (The Resistance, 2009-09-11). This felt especially appropriate for the conclusion of the first “failure branch”, after which the player is given another chance to get it right. “MB” is, of course, songwriter/vocalist/guitarist/keyboardist Matthew Bellamy.

Core Done Blew

Title

A pun on cordon bleu, French for blue ribbon. In English, this phrase most commonly refers to:

  • a skilful chef
  • an award given to such chefs
  • a form of cooking in which thin slices of various kinds of meat are breaded and sautéed

This style of cooking isn’t popular everywhere, so some players may not get the pun.

Dormīre, fortuītō somniāre
(To Sleep, Perchance to Dream)

Title

The level title, of course, comes from Hamlet’s famous soliloquy in William Shakespeare’s Hamlet (c. 1600), which is only the greatest literary work in English history. You may recognize the sources of several other titles within this soliloquy (including a level title from Marathon 2).

To be, or not to be, that is the Question:
Whether ’tis Nobler in the mind to suffer
The Slings and Arrows of outrageous Fortune,
Or to take Arms against a Sea of troubles,
And by opposing end them. To die, to sleep
No more; and by a sleep to say we end
The heart-ache and the thousand Natural shocks
That Flesh is heir to? ’Tis a consummation
Devoutly to be wish’d. To die, to sleep;
To sleep, perchance to Dream: ay, there’s the rub;
For in that sleep of death what dreams may come,
When we have shuffl’d off this mortal coil,
Must give us pause—there’s the respect
That makes Calamity of so long life;
For who would bear the Whips and Scorns of time,
Th’oppressor’s wrong, the proud man’s Contumely,
The pangs of dispriz’d Love, the Law’s delay,
The insolence of Office, and the Spurns
That patient merit of th’unworthy takes,
When he himself might his Quietus make
With a bare Bodkin? Who would Fardels bear,
To grunt and sweat under a weary life,
But that the dread of something after death,
The undiscover’d Country, from whose Bourn
No Traveller returns, Puzzles the will,
And makes us rather bear those ills we have
Than fly to others that we know not of?
Thus Conscience does make Cowards of us all,
And thus the Native hue of Resolution
Is sicklied o’er with the pale cast of Thought,
And enterprises of great pitch and moment
With this regard their Currents turn awry,
And lose the name of Action.—Soft you now!
The fair Ophelia! Nymph, in thy Orisons
Be all my sins remember’d.

Star Trek VI: The Undiscovered Country (1991-12-06), the British dream pop band This Mortal Coil, the Richard Matheson novel What Dreams May Come (1978-09), and Arcturus’ song “The Bodkin & the Quietus (…To Reach the Stars)” (Aspera hiems symfonia, 1996-06-03) are all titled, at least in part, after phrases from this soliloquy.

Again, crude Hellenizations of:

  • the title, a Greek word evidently coined by the Macedonian anthologist Johannes Stobaeus (Ἰωάννης ὁ Στοβαῖος, Iōánnēs ho Stobaîos, John the Stobian; Stobi may have been the city he was born or lived in, but we know nothing about him, not even when he lived). It merges ἐνᾰντῐ́ος (enăntĭ́os, opposite) and δρόμος (drómos, racetrack) to describe an observation from the pre-Socratic philosopher Heraclitus (Ἡράκλειτος, Hērákleitos) that many phenomena in nature turn into their opposites, especially in the absence of balance
  • “Designed by”
  • and our names.
