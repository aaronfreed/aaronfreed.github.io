Overview

Q: What is this?

A: An overview of level titles and map writing in Eternal X ⟨eternal.bungie.org⟩, a scenario for Bungie’s Marathon trilogy ⟨alephone.lhowon.org⟩ (1994-1996, not to be confused with the 2026 extraction shooter of the same name, although they do take place in the same setting… but probably not the same timeline. Probably).

When I refer to “map writing“, by the way, I’m referring to disconnected lines that the game itself mostly ignores. These can only be seen in a map editor like Weland ⟨github.com/treellama/weland/releases⟩ (whose documentation is the sort only a Programmer™ could write; see my setup guide for MacOS and Windows ⟨aaronfreed.github.io/mapmaking101.html⟩ if you have trouble on either OS).

Q: Why does it exist?

A: I wanted it catalogued somewhere. Some mad lad put a lot of this info up on TV Tropes ⟨tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/GratuitousLatin/MarathonExpandedUniverse⟩, but commentary from one of a scenario’s lead developers on their own website is a lot more permanent and authoritative than a wiki anyone can edit.

Q: Why does so very much of the text use foreign languages (with questionable grammatical validity)?

A: I like foreign languages, especially Latin and Greek – which I’ve never had formal training in, so I’m sure I’ve missed some important concepts. (Well, I say that, but English is really just Latin, Greek, French, and German in a trenchcoat pretending to be a real language ⟨aaronfreed.github.io/germanic-romance.html⟩, so in a way, I have been trained in them my whole life.)

Q: Why does so much of the map text consist of song lyrics translated into foreign languages?

A: Autism. (I tried giving a more detailed answer, but it condensed to literally just “autism“.)

Q: Some of the map writing mentioned here either isn’t present in my version or doesn’t match what’s there.

A: That’s not a question.

Q: Why is some of the map writing mentioned here either not present or different in my version?

A: It’s based on the current development branch, which may or may not get released as a new preview sometime this decade. I’m mostly basing it on the development branch due to the second, secret reason I’m keeping this: to remind my future self what I was thinking those many years ago. (Wait, guess my secret’s out now.)

Q: Why does most of the numbering start with zero?

A: Eternal’s level and chapter numbering both start at zero. It only seemed fair for the rest of the table of contents to follow suit. (I currently have no information about the titles of levels whose sub-list items start at one.)

Q: Should readers know anything else about this before we begin?

A:

  • There are spoilers in here, and not just for Eternal. I haven’t catalogued them all yet. If you’re the sort of person that gets upset by spoilers, you’ll probably want to wait until I’ve done that.

    I don’t think there’s anything on the level of what I term ontological spoilers, which are spoilers that completely recontextualize a work. These are so powerful that even knowing that they exist can be dangerous, but two examples of works with ontological spoilers are the TV show The Good Place and Steven Erikson’s fantasy series The Malazan Book of the Fallen.

    But that said, what qualifies as an ontological spoiler is subjective. I personally don’t think a ten-minute song can have an ontological spoiler, but if you do, you won’t want to read a word I’ve written about Genesis’ “One for the Vine” until you’ve listened to it and absorbed its lyrics.

    (I do think a ninety-minute concept album like The Lamb Lies Down on Broadway can have an ontological spoiler – which it does. I suspect the difference is rooted not in length but in word count. “One for the Vine” has 316 words and 1,529 characters. By the end of The Lamb Lies Down on Broadway’s first song, which is less than half as long, it’s almost matched the former’s word count and blown clean past its character count. But like I said, it’s subjective, and I’ll be the first to admit my standards on this are arbitrary.)

  • …Parental advisory warning for occasional foul language and discussions of violence and adult topics? (Kids probably shouldn’t play the Marathon trilogy or its scenarios anyway: all three were rated “Mature” when they came out, for what that’s worth. And if you haven’t played Eternal, I’m not sure why this interests you.)

That’s all I can think of.

Eternal X

Prōlogus: Omnia aut nihil, vel: Fātum
(Prologue: All or Nothing, or: Destiny)

To my disappointment, destiny was our only original chapter title that didn’t have an exact cognate in Latin with an identical meaning. Destiny is actually the noun form of the verb destine, meaning the state of being destined or the act of destining; destine in turn is derived from the Latin verb dēstinō. It displaced the Old English word wyrd, which was in turn an ancestor of (and was pronounced identically to) Modern English weird. Dēstinō is also etymologically related to dēstinātiō, whence destination.

Unfortunately, no suitable noun form of dēstinō means destiny, so I instead used fātum – which has its own interesting etymology, but that’s another story.

I mentioned “original chapter title” since Pfhorrest came up with alternate chapter titles for the prologue and epilogue during 1.3’s development: all or nothing for the prologue, dialectical synthesis for the epilogue. As seen above, Latin has two different words that can translate as or, but like the two Greek words for ignorance, they’re not precisely synonyms. Saying that aut is either/or and vel is and/or might be glib, but it’s also basically correct.

Lātus procul nūllius locī

Title

My non-literal Latin translation of “The Far Side of Nowhere”. It literally means “The Far Side of No Place”, but in Latin phrases like this, it’s best to use the genitive case, which nusquam (nowhere), being an adverb, does not have. “Lātus procul dē nusquam” would be a literal (and more awkward) translation.

Σηπτοκοκκική Φφορυγγίτιδα
(Septococcal Pfhoryngitis)

Title

The map name is a pun on the phrase streptococcal pharyngitis, or strep throat. Streptococcal is derived from στρεπτός (streptós, or twisted), and κόκκος (kókkos, or seed). Pharyngitis is derived from φάρυγξ (phárynx, or throat) and -ῖτις (-îtis), which denoted a connection to a place in ancient Greek, but has become -ίτιδα (-ítida) in modern Greek, denoting inflammations. There’s an additional pun here on σηπτικός (sēptikós, whence septic), meaning characterized by putridity.

Δυσμεντρία
(Dysmentria)

Title

“Dysmentria” is a pun on two Ancient Greek words:

  • Δυσμετρία (dysmetría): dysmetria, meaning an inability to control or limit muscular movements;
  • Μένος (ménos): mind, desire, anger, violence, etc.

Thus, it might be interpreted as meaning an inability to limit or control one’s mind, desire, anger, or violence.

An acutal ancient Greek word related to this concept is ἀκράτεια (romanized akráteia) or ἀκρασίᾱ (romanized akrasíā), which exists in modern English under the spellings akrasia and acrasia, meaning a lack of physical or (especially) mental strength, poor willpower, and a tendency to act against one’s better judgement.

Sakhmet Rising

Title

A reference to Egyptian mythology. Sakhmet (also seen spelled as Sekhmet, Sachmis, Scheme, and others) is an aspect of Hathor (or, in some traditions, Bast) that emerges when she is sufficiently angered. Much later (in “Bug-Eyed in Space” and “Once More Unto the Breach”), Leela explains:

Sakhmet served as protector of the pharaohs and led them in warfare, was called upon to ward off disease, subdued Ra’s enemies as the Eye of Ra, and was known as “One Before Whom Evil Trembles”. But she also had many much darker aspects, as suggested by her other titles, “Mistress of Dread”, “Lady of Slaughter”, and “She Who Mauls”.

The gods once unleased Sakhmet to punish humanity, but quickly regretted it, as her rampage far exceeded their intentions; her bloodthirst (which is not a metaphor) grew with each person she slaughtered, until she threatened to wipe out humanity entirely. Her rampage was only placated when Ra filled a lake with beer he’d dyed red to resemble blood. She drank all of it, which got her so drunk she gave up the slaughter and returned peacefully to being Hathor.

Players are invited to draw their own conclusions.

“Nōnne potes mē pulsantēm audīre?”
In memoriam C. Watts (MCMXLI–MMXXI)

A tribute to Rolling Stones drummer Charlie Watts, who passed away in 2021; I was working on this map when I found out and just wrote a tribute. The first line literally means “Are you not able to hear my knocking?”, a reference to “Can’t You Hear Me Knocking?”, a Sticky Fingers (1971-04-23) track with a stunning spontaneous jam that, according to legend, erupted after guitarist Mick Taylor simply kept playing after the song ended. Watts, bassist Bill Wyman, and conga player Rocky Dijon lay down an absolute monster of a groove throughout the entire track, which is easily my favorite Stones song.

(I do feel at least Taylor should be credited as a cowriter along Mick Jagger and Keith Richards. Cases could also be made for everyone who contributed to the jam: Watts, Wyman, Dijon, saxophonist Bobby Keys, organist Billy Preston, and percussionist/producer Jimmy Miller.)

Dormīre, fortuītō somniāre
(To Sleep, Perchance to Dream)

Title

The level title, of course, comes from Hamlet’s famous soliloquy in William Shakespeare’s Hamlet (c. 1600), which is only the greatest literary work in English history. You may recognize the sources of several other titles within this soliloquy (including a level title from Marathon 2).

To be, or not to be, that is the Question:
Whether ’tis Nobler in the mind to suffer
The Slings and Arrows of outrageous Fortune,
Or to take Arms against a Sea of troubles,
And by opposing end them. To die, to sleep
No more; and by a sleep to say we end
The heart-ache and the thousand Natural shocks
That Flesh is heir to? ’Tis a consummation
Devoutly to be wish’d. To die, to sleep;
To sleep, perchance to Dream: ay, there’s the rub;
For in that sleep of death what dreams may come,
When we have shuffl’d off this mortal coil,
Must give us pause—there’s the respect
That makes Calamity of so long life;
For who would bear the Whips and Scorns of time,
Th’oppressor’s wrong, the proud man’s Contumely,
The pangs of dispriz’d Love, the Law’s delay,
The insolence of Office, and the Spurns
That patient merit of th’unworthy takes,
When he himself might his Quietus make
With a bare Bodkin? Who would Fardels bear,
To grunt and sweat under a weary life,
But that the dread of something after death,
The undiscover’d Country, from whose Bourn
No Traveller returns, Puzzles the will,
And makes us rather bear those ills we have
Than fly to others that we know not of?
Thus Conscience does make Cowards of us all,
And thus the Native hue of Resolution
Is sicklied o’er with the pale cast of Thought,
And enterprises of great pitch and moment
With this regard their Currents turn awry,
And lose the name of Action.—Soft you now!
The fair Ophelia! Nymph, in thy Orisons
Be all my sins remember’d.

Star Trek VI: The Undiscovered Country (1991-12-06), the British dream pop band This Mortal Coil, the Richard Matheson novel What Dreams May Come (1978-09), and Arcturus’ song “The Bodkin & the Quietus (…To Reach the Stars)” (Aspera hiems symfonia, 1996-06-03) are all titled, at least in part, after phrases from this soliloquy.

Ἐναντιοδρομία
(Enantiodromia)

Title

A Greek word for running in opposite ways evidently coined by the Macedonian anthologist Johannes Stobaeus (Ἰωάννης ὁ Στοβαῖος, Iōánnēs ho Stobaîos, John the Stobian; Stobi may have been the city he was born or lived in, but we know nothing about him, not even when he lived). It merges ἐναντίος (enantíos, opposite) and δρόμος (drómos, racetrack) to describe an observation from the pre-Socratic philosopher Heraclitus (Ἡράκλειτος, Hērákleitos) that many phenomena in nature turn into their opposites, especially in the absence of balance: as Heraclitus wrote, “Cold things warm, warm things cool, wet things dry, and parched things get wet.”

More recently, Friedrich Nietzsche and Carl Jung re-popularized the concept. Jung’s concept of the shadow is an influence on our characterization of Hathor: the shadow isn’t evil, precisely, but it isn’t always pleasant. The shadow is also an influence on “Hi Ren”, the song quoted in the map writing.

This is also the title of the first song on The Long Defeat (2022-03-23), the French avant-garde black metal band Deathspell Omega’s eighth album. I reviewed most of their work ⟨aaronfreed.github.io/reviews/deathspellomega.html⟩ in 2016, which I’ve since expanded to address their two latest albums’ lyrics and to translate their Latin and Greek text. Heraclitus’ concept, a central theme of Eternal long before The Long Defeat, is this title’s primary referent, but the song can be seen as a secondary one.

Other cultures, whether or not they have words for enantiodromia, are certainly familiar with the concept. For example, the fourteenth-century Chinese novel Romance of the Three Kingdoms (三國演義), traditionally attributed to Luo Guanzhong (羅貫中), opens:「話說天下大勢，分久必合，合久必分。」(Whether or not Guanzhong wrote Romance, he certainly didn’t write that line, which was added centuries later.)

This line is impossible to translate precisely, but one of the most popular translations is Moss Roberts’ “Here begins our tale. The empire, long divided, must unite; long united, must divide. Thus it has ever been.” Which is concise, but takes a few artistic liberties and fails to convey the whole meaning.

“It is said to be the fate of all things under the heavens: what is long divided shall unite, what is long united shall divide” is vastly more complete and closer to literally accurate, but fails to express the original’s poetry. (“All things under the heavens” is also both a wordy and imperfect rendering of「天下大勢」, which Roberts translates as empire, and which, within the context of the novel alone, can be interpreted to mean not just the Han empire but also families, friendships, romantic relationships, political alliances, and even mental health. Outside the novel’s context, it can be interpreted even more broadly.)

No translation can possibly convey all of an original text’s intended meanings to another language, and it’s often necessary to invent new ones that may not even have been intended by the original author. (To be fair, this sort of thing inevitably happens even in the original language, as usage invariably changes over time.) But even by Classical Chinese standards, this is a particularly vexing passage to translate.

The important point here, though, is that it is a clear example of enantiodromia, and a particularly famous one, not even just within China but worldwide.

They’d be less likely to be omitted if they were in, say, the perfect tense and/or the subjunctive mood, and the surrounding context gave no indication that they should be interpreted as such.     1. “I am he, the chosen one” in the first verse parallels “This is he, God’s chosen one” in the second chorus.
    2. “He never voiced [the former claim] loud” because the latter’s believers talked him into making it.
    3. “Then one whose faith had died / Fled back up the mountainside” in the first chorus parallels “Then, on a distant slope / He observed one without hope / Flee back up the mountainside” in the final chorus.
    4. He “vanished into air” in the final chorus, and the tribespeople “saw him appear” in the third verse (or second, in sources that number the first two verses as a single verse).
    5.     6. The song’s message hits even harder if the epiphany he has at the end of the song is that the entire reason he was displaced from everything he ever knew was because he was fleeing himself.
    7. Tony Banks is a known Doctor Who fan.

    Kurt Vonnegut’s onomatopoeia of birdsong in Slaughterhouse-Five (1969-03-31). Vonnegut addressed his publisher in its metafictional opening chapter, explaining:

    Sam, here’s the book.

    It is so short and jumbled and jangled, Sam, because there is nothing intelligent to say about a massacre. Everybody is supposed to be dead, to never say anything or want anything ever again. Everything is supposed to be very quiet after a massacre, and it always is, except for the birds.

    And what do the birds say? All there is to say about a massacre, things like “Poo-tee-weet?”

    I have told my sons that they are not under any circumstances to take part in massacres, and that the news of massacres of enemies is not to fill them with satisfaction or glee.

    I have also told them not to work for companies which make massacre machinery, and to express contempt for people who think we need machinery like that.

    “Poo-tee-weet?” is also the final line of the book.

    People often accuse Kurt Vonnegut of fatalism. If these people read Slaughterhouse-Five, they read it literally, but they didn’t read it carefully. His words to his children aren’t the words of a fatalist. Nor are:

    My father died many years ago now—of natural causes. So it goes. He was a sweet man. He was a gun nut, too. He left me his guns. They rust.

    Billy Pilgrim is a fatalist, but Billy Pilgrim is not Kurt Vonnegut, as the book’s fifth chapter explicitly clarifies: after Pilgrim encounters an American suffering an unspeakable stomach ailment, Vonnegut writes, “That was I. That was me. That was the author of this book.”

    Besides the first chapter and part of the last, nearly everything in the book is what Pilgrim says happened to him, but Pilgrim is also an unreliable narrator. He has untreated (and quite possibly undiagnosed) posttraumatic stress disorder (hence all the flashbacks) and has developed some form of hallucination as a coping mechanism. None of this makes him a bad person, to be clear; it simply means he needs help.

    If Vonnegut meant us to sympathize with the perspective of this novel’s Tralfamadorians, why does he have them blow up the universe in an accident and shrug it off along the lines of, “Oh well, that was always meant to happen?” Which is quite similar to the stance of our ascended Jjaro, I might note, and beyond its antiwar themes, Vonnegut’s antiauthoritarian, anticapitalist stance is quite similar to ours.

    All these similarities are mostly coincidence, since Pfhorrest hadn’t read it while writing most of Eternal’s plot, but I’ve played up some similarities up in recent revisions. The entire reason this stage plays the loon sound effect, in fact, is so that, after the enemies are dead, the only sounds besides the wind crashing against the rocks and the rushing of water will be birdsong.

    It may also be relevant to note that five brief levels in Marathon Chronicles, my unfinished scenario that is partly a sequel to Eternal, are named “Slaughterhouse-One” through “Slaughterhouse-Five”.

    All that said, I believe that to read Slaughterhouse-Five as science-fiction is to fundamentally misread it. It is a metafictional novel that contains some science-fiction elements, almost all of which appear as part of a tragic story about a man trying (and mostly failing) to cope with combat trauma. Like most Vonnegut books, it also contains actual science-fiction by his alter-ego Kilgore Trout. (His piscine surname was inspired by Vonnegut’s friend Theodore Sturgeon. If I ever develop an alter-ego, he’ll probably be named Mike Bream, for baseball players Sid Bream and Mike Trout, thus continuing a tradition of ichthyic alter-egos. I also like to believe that Kilgore was Mike’s great-uncle.)

    The final words of Yes’ “Awaken” (Going for the One, 1977-07-12), with the bracketed words being additions to make the meaning clearer in Latin. This is a stealth pun, because the song title, in a single word, describes exactly what the player is about to do after this level ends.

  3. Possumus ambulāre viam nostram idipsum
    si ambitiōnēs nostrae sunt omnēs eādem.
    Possumus currere sōlī līberīque
    si persequimur propositum differentem.
    Illūminētur amōris vēritātem.
    Lūceat clāram vēritātis amōrem.
    Sēnsibilitās, armāta cum sēnsū lībertāteque,
    cum corde menteque ūnītā in ūnā sphaerā perfectā.
    –NP, MCMLXXVII

    Translation:

    We can walk a road together
    if our goals are all the same.
    We can run alone and free
    if we pursue a different aim.
    Let the truth of love be lighter.
    Let the love of truth shine clear.
    Sensibility, armed with sense and liberty,
    with the heart and mind united in a single perfect sphere.
    –Neil Peart, 1977

    From Rush’s “Cygnus X-1, Book II: Hemispheres” (Hemispheres, 1978-10-24), the sort of song title that could only belong to a side-length progressive rock epic. This song represents the start of Peart’s decisive break with Ayn Rand, one of his foundational philosophical influences on earlier albums.

    Its core argument is that both reason and love are needed for a balanced worldview. Apollo gives the people the gift of technology that protects them from the elements, but without emotional attachments, their lives are empty and unfulfilling. Dionysus gives them the gift of love, but they’re unable to fend for themselves in the winter. Followers of the two end up fighting for an unspecified amount of time before the protagonist, who’d flown into the eponymous black hole at the end of Book I, screams in despair at seeing the fighting. Dionysus and Apollo reconsider their stance, name the explorer Cygnus, and dub him the god of balance.

    Rand, who wrote essays with titles like The Virtue of Selfishness, would have probably regarded this with the same disparaging attitude in which I hold her works. Peart would break from her even more decisively on future albums, e.g. Grace Under Pressure, which battles it out with Hemispheres and Moving Pictures for the honor of my favorite Rush album.

    Balance is also the central theme of Eternal, although it is more focused on the balance between order and chaos. In “We Met Once in the Garden”, Hathor writes:

    Ancient Egyptian religion was concerned less with good and evil than with order and chaos. It also had a concept (and goddess) called ma’at, a synthesis of order, balance, and justice. (In some myths, Ra and my namesake were the goddess Ma’at’s parents.) The ascended Jjaro sought to embody order, and the W’rkncacnter chaos. But in their futile quest to impose order on an entropic universe, the abyss has gazed into the ascended Jjaro, who have by now become enantiodromic mirrors of their foes. Their conflict has caused the impending explosion of the galaxy – thereby handing victory to the very W’rkncacnter they claim to oppose. They have forgotten ma’at.

    Ma’at comes from living in harmony with nature and with each other, from being benevolent and kind, from being honourable and truthful, from alleviating the suffering of those less fortunate than us rather than exploiting their misfortune. Order cannot exist without balance or justice, and trying too hard to impose it results in chaos, just as trying too hard to fit a square peg into a round hole breaks the peg.

    […]

    The closest thing Ma’at had to an opposite was the deity Apep. Where ma’at was a force of creation, Apep was one of destruction. Apep was the only deity the Egyptians prayed against; an incomprehensible being of pure chaos that resented reality and sought to unmake it. Apep couldn’t be permanently defeated by any known means, and it sought to unmake the sun, which set because Ra had to fight Apep every night.

    Now, that all sounds a bit familiar, no? Being of chaos, resents our reality’s existence, seeks only to destroy? Sounds a bit like the W’rkncacnter, whom I shall now call Apep for simplicity’s sake. Telling Apep that destroying our galaxy won’t bring its reality back would be as futile as telling Sakhmet that her quest for revenge was futile would’ve been. A man hears what he wants to hear and disregards the rest.

    More interesting to me is how Apep and the ascended Jjaro came to mirror each other. To the latter, the W’rkncacnter are disrupting what they’ve come to regard as the natural order, while Apep sees them – and our entire species – as usurping it. Protest as they might, their clash is not a fundamental philosophical disagreement, but a mere fight to emerge atop a hierarchy. Both take as granted that in-groups will be protected but not bound by the law, and that out-groups will be bound but not protected by it. They are merely fighting to be the in-group. They may see each other as opposites, but they are merely two shades of tyranny.

    The logical counterproposal, of course, is that the law must equally both protect and bind everyone, including leaders. No leader can truly embody the balance or justice of ma’at unless bound by the law.

    I wrote Hathor’s “We Met Once in the Garden” terminal, and it’s probably little shock that she speaks for me in the vast majority of it, or that Pfhorrest also largely agrees. My first book, On Moonlight: a film analysis, autobiography, and holistic social critique, contains sections on Egyptian religion #10216;aaronfreed.github.io/moonlight.html#dikaiosynekaiisotes⟩ and why we should revive the concept of ma’at. #10216;aaronfreed.github.io/moonlight.html#pantestyrannoi⟩

    The bit about out-groups and in-groups paraphrases a comment by composer Frank Wilhoit to the website Crooked Timber, which I reprint and comment on #10216;aaronfreed.github.io/moonlight.html#wilhoit⟩ in On Moonlight. I don’t agree with every word he writes, but I don’t need to; his main point, “The law cannot protect anyone unless it binds everyone, and it cannot bind anyone unless it protects everyone,” has been a cornerstone of my political philosophy for decades.

    I consider the ascended Jjaro to represent not order, but another form of chaos that habitually masquerades as order (chaos isn’t a viable electoral strategy). “Order” without justice or balance is intrinsically unstable, and instability isn’t order. It’s chaos dressing up as order for Halloween. Thus, the ascended Jjaro’s conflict with Apep isn’t order versus chaos: it’s chaos versus chaos. Order is found in the middle, when we purge tyrants of all sorts and replace them with panarchy, or rule by all.

    (One of the best studies I’ve found of how tyranny is chaos is The Furnaces of Palingenesia (2019-05-24), Deathspell Omega’s seventh album, presented as a manifesto by a nameless leader for a political faction known only by the ironic name of the Order, which wipes out all traces of life by the end of the album. See the section on the title of “Enantiodromia” for more about Deathspell Omega.)

    This is all a very long aside to say I included the Rush quote because it beautifully reflects Eternal’s central theme. The balance between order and chaos, in a sense, can be considered true order, which lies between a form of chaos found in both Eternal’s Apep and its namesake (chaotic evil, in Dungeons & Dragons terms, which never pretends to be anything but chaos) and the ascended Jjaro and the Order’s chaos (lawful evil, which pretends to be order, but is just another form of chaos). Ma’at and true order are neutral good.

  4. Numquam verō erat proelium prō mē vincere. Erat saltātiō aeterna. Ac sicuti aliqua saltãtiō, rigidior fīēbam, difficilior saltãtiō fīēbat. Magis exsecrābar vestīgia inconcinna mea, magis lūctābar. Igitur adolēscēbam, ac discēbam mollīre, ac illa saltātiō fīēbat facilior.

    Est haec saltātiō aeterna quae dīvidit hominēs ab angelīs, ab daemonibus, ab dīs. Ac debeō nē oblīvīscī, dēbēmus nē oblīvīscī, ut sumus hominēs.

    –RG, MMXXII

    Last words of “Hi Ren” (this monologue’s first half is written on “Enantiodromia”).

    One thing many reviewers missed about this song is that Ren does not defeat his shadow. He assimilates it. Throughout the song, whenever the shadow speaks, the lights flicker. When his conscious self speaks by itself, they don’t. But, in the final moments of the song before the monologue, the lights are still flickering. Ren has integrated his shadow.

    We spend most of Eternal chasing Hathor, believing she is the root of our problems. As the game progresses, we learn that they run much deeper, and that she’s actually being manipulated to more sinister ends by other factions that do have malevolent motives. At the end of the game, she expresses sincere remorse and leaves the door open to reconciliation.

    If you interpret Rubicon as being in the same universe, she may be feeding Tycho information. She has certainly fed us information in secret terminals (in “Dysmentria” and “The Near Side of Everywhere”) in a way that suggests that she sincerely wishes to assist us however she can.

    I don’t believe Hathor originally was intentionally written as a Jungian archetype. But there are certainly similarities, and I’ve played them up somewhat in recent plot revisions. A necessary step to inner peace is learning to treat our shadows with a level head rather than fight, try to suppress, or simply yield to them.

    In “We Met Once in the Garden”, the appropriate response to Hathor’s attacks isn’t to fight back – that just prolongs the fight. Nor is it to yield. It’s to wield one of the Jjaro weapons and simply stand tall. After a time, she tires of attacking, flies off, and sends a message expressing sincere remorse for her actions throughout the game. Why bring this fight up now, while discussing the Jungian shadow, instead of in the section for “We Met Once in the Garden”? You may interpret that as you will. That approach feels appropriately Jungian.

    In any case, “Hi Ren” perfectly encapsulates many of Eternal’s themes, and it’s the profoundest statement on mental health I’ve seen this entire century, so I’ll give Ren the last words (credit and navigation bar aside):

    It was never really a battle for me to win. It was an eternal dance. And like any dance, the more rigid I became, the harder it got. The more I cursed my clumsy footsteps, the more I struggled. So I got older, and I learned to soften, and that dance got easier.

    It is this eternal dance that separates human beings from angels, from demons, from gods. And I must not forget, we must not forget, that we are human beings.

    –Ren Gill, 2022
