Explanation & Criteria

Pop songs I consider beyond criticism, chronological order, limit two songs per artist. Criteria:

  1. Not a note must feel out of place.
  2. The arrangement and production quality must be immaculate by contemporary standards.
  3. The melodies must be catchy enough to get stuck in your head for days.
  4. They must also be good enough not to get annoying when they do.
  5. The performances must be consistently great.
  6. The lyrics must at least not be completely asinine.
  7. The songs must be commercially successful and/or significantly influence future music.
  8. The songs must rank among their respective artists’ best pop songs.

Caveats, Qualifications, & Trivia

A few caveats & qualifications, & trivia:

  • I differentiate “best song” and “best pop song”, e.g., while I consider “Atmosphere” Joy Division’s best song, it’s too slow and moody to go on this list; Beyoncé’s best song may well be “Formation”, but it’s too unconventional for this list. Are “Friday I’m in Love”, “Touch of Grey”, “Nothing Else Matters”, or “Creep” the Cure, Grateful Dead, Metallica, or Radiohead’s best songs? No. Are they their best pop songs? Yes. I often listed acts’ signature songs (or one of them, if they had two or more) unless lesser-known songs were so much more acclaimed (e.g., “Toxic”) or hit so much harder (e.g., “The Night They Drove Old Dixie Down”, “Someone Saved My Life Tonight”, “Synchronicity II”) that I couldn’t justify listing a bigger hit.
  • This should go without saying, but I’m not endorsing the creators of every song on this list; e.g., Phil Spector was violent, unstable, and abusive even before committing murder – and a brilliant producer. As much as we might wish otherwise, artistic skill is not always correlated with being a good person (insofar as “good person” is even a valid concept – I would argue that acts and beliefs can be good or bad, but the vast majority of people are far too complicated to sum up with such dualistic labels. Exceptions: monsters on the order of King Leopold II, Adolf Hitler, Josef Stalin, Mao Zedong, or Pol Pot, who are, thankfully, extremely rare).
  • Likewise, while completely asinine lyrics disqualify a song, I don’t always approve of every word of each song on this list (e.g., I have no sympathy for the Lost Cause of the South, but “The Night They Drove Old Dixie Down” is still a masterpiece. Being an antiwar song above all else does help its case).
  • I’m not comparing these songs’ quality. I consider them all some variety of pop, but a lot of these are soccer-and-hockey comparisons: same goal (put round object in net), totally different game.
  • Usually, I list a song’s first release date, whether that was on an album or as a single. Occasionally, however, a remake or live performance is such a vast improvement on the original version that I go with that instead (e.g., “Hello It’s Me”, “I Want You to Want Me”, “Take on Me”).
  • My definition of “pop” is somewhat expansive. I foresee objections that some of these songs are really rock (“Dream On”, “Sympathy for the Devil”, “Nothing Else Matters”), soul (“A Change Is Gonna Come”, “People Get Ready”, “I Got You (I Feel Good)”), folk (“California Dreamin’”, “Big Yellow Taxi”), country (“Wichita Lineman”, “Wicked Game”), disco (“Stayin’ Alive”, “Lowdown”, “Dancing Queen”), post-punk (“Love Will Tear Us Apart”, “There Is a Light That Never Goes Out”), jazz (“What a Wonderful World”, “Feels So Good”), alternative (“Velouria”, “Smells Like Teen Spirit”, “Creep”), psychedelic (“Little Wing”, “Eight Miles High”, “White Rabbit”), progressive (“I’m Not in Love”, “Bohemian Rhapsody”, “November Rain”), or hip-hop (“Hey Ya!”, “Crazy in Love”). I’m not overly interested in genre arguments. Did it receive lots of radio airplay? Did it sell lots of copies? Is it at least somewhat melodically and harmonically conventional? If the answer to all three is “yes”, then I consider it pop. (Someday, I may break this list down into subgenres, but I’ve spent long enough on it as it is. It doesn’t help that many songs could fit into multiple subgenres.)
  • If I don’t know a song’s exact release date, my policy is to sort it to the middle of the month. As it happens, March 1975 is the only month on this list with this issue; I sort “I’m Not in Love” to 1975-03-16.
  • If the number 128 seems arbitrary, bear in mind that it is 2⁷, and powers of two hold a lot of meaning to me because I grew up with computers, which use powers of two everywhere. It is arbitrary, mind you: I had to choose some number as a cutoff point, and 128 seemed as good a number as any.

Additional Notes

  • “Running Up That Hill (A Deal with God)” is the greatest pop song of my lifetime; “Dancing Queen” is the greatest pop song of all time. (I will die on this hill. Before you ask, I have no idea if it’s the one Kate Bush wants to run up.) “Good Vibrations”, “What’s Going On”, and “Bridge Over Troubled Water” follow “Dancing Queen” on my list; I haven’t decided which, if any, I rank above “Running Up That Hill”. (Quality, despite my facetious subtitle, is much too subjective to be easily used as a criterion by which to order a list.)
  • After exhaustive investigation, my friends and I have concluded that “Dancing Queen” is the only song ever actually written about dancing; all others use dancing as a metaphor for sex. This is part of why I rank it as the greatest pop song ever written. (Another reason: it’s timeless. It sounds like it could’ve been written in 1700, and it sounds like it could’ve been written yesterday. This is not an exhaustive list of reasons.)

  • Only Marvin Gaye, the Beatles, the Rolling Stones, Aretha Franklin, & Beyoncé have two songs on this list, though a few additional artists are connected to varying extents:

    • The Supremes, Diana Ross: Ross fronted the Supremes before starting a solo career
    • Boz Scaggs, Toto: three future Toto members performed on Scaggs’ “Lowdown”, one of them cowrote it, and those sessions ultimately led to Toto’s formation
    • Genesis, Phil Collins, Peter Gabriel: Collins was Genesis’ second lead singer and drummer; Gabriel was their original lead singer (but left before they wrote and recorded their song on this list)
    • Bob Dylan, the Band: The Band were famously Bob Dylan’s backing band, although that occurred between his song on this list and theirs
  • Only “Bridge Over Troubled Water” appears in versions by two performers. I’m not sure which version I like more, but when I realized I considered two versions good enough to list here, and that this was true of no other song, that convinced me it’s one of the five best pop songs ever written.

  • A possibly incomplete list of additional songwriters with multiple entries on this list who don’t have multiple entries as performers (or band members, e.g., Lennon/McCartney, Jagger/Richards):

    • Otis Redding: “Respect”, “(Sittin’ on) the Dock of the Bay”
    • Brian Holland, Lamont Dozier, & Eddie Holland: “Where Did Our Love Go”, “Reach Out (I’ll Be There)”
    • Burt Bacharach & Hal David: “What the World Needs Now Is Love”, “(They Long to Be) Close to You”
    • Smokey Robinson: “My Girl” (with Ronald White) & “The Tracks of My Tears”
    • David Paich: “Lowdown” (with Boz Scaggs) & “Africa” (with Jeff Porcaro)

  • The fact that 1967 tops this list with nine songs will probably surprise very few people. I may not agree that music as a whole peaked in 1967, but I can certainly agree that pop music did. 1965 being in second place with eight songs probably also won’t surprise many people, nor will 1971 being tied for third (especially if they’ve seen the Apple TV+ documentary 1971: The Year That Music Changed Everything).

    What might surprise people is the year 1971 is tied with: 1991. If you’d asked me to predict that, I might’ve guessed 1972, 1973, 1980, or 1989. I also wouldn’t have predicted that 1992 would be in fifth place with six entries, that 1974 wouldn’t have any entries at all, or that 2003 would have five, putting it in a three-way tie for sixth place with 1968 and 1978. (1978 is part of its own mini-surprise: I might’ve predicted that it’d have a lot of entries because disco peaked around then, but only two of its five songs are disco.)

    I won’t snobbishly claim later decades’ underrepresentation is a sign of pop music’s decline, though: it’s a sign that I stopped listening to the radio sometime around 1998. This breakdown is in no way an objective measurement of pop music’s quality, anyway; it’s not really even a subjective measurement, since I arbitrarily limited this list to at most two songs per artist (and then even more arbitrarily decided to limit most artists to one song). If I were to compile a complete list of every pop song I considered perfect, it’d be far closer to being a subjective measurement of quality, but don’t hold your breath waiting for that to happen.

  • “Someday We’ll Be Together”, officially credited to Diana Ross & the Supremes, is in reality Ross’ debut solo single: the backing vocalists, rather than being Mary Wilson and Cindy Birdsong (the other Supremes at the time), are Merry Clayton, Julia Waters Tillman, Maxine Waters Willard, and Johnny Bristol.
  • “Big Yellow Taxi” and “What a Fool Believes” top the list of “songs that sound happy, but are actually horrendously depressing once you pay attention to the lyrics”. Weirdly, Mitchell’s jaunty arrangement actually feels more depressing than Counting Crows and Vanessa Carlton’s comparatively somber arrangement in their 2002 cover, which I think is because the lyrics make Mitchell’s performance feel sarcastic. (I enjoy both versions, to the extent that one can “enjoy” such a depressing song.)
  • “Take on Me” and “Golden” very likely share the award for “most diabolically unsingable Billboard #1s in history”, each requiring a thirty-one-semitone (two octaves and a perfect fifth) vocal range. “Take on Me” spans A₂ (the chorus’ first note) to E₅ (its last note); “Golden” spans D₃ (the bridge’s low note) to A₅ (the chorus’ high notes). “Golden” is arguably slightly harder, since its high notes are sung in a mixed belt, while “Take on Me” switches to falsetto for its high notes. Regardless, they’re both infamously difficult to sing.
  • I’ve only confirmed one pair of songs on this list to have been released on the exact same day (1992-11-02): “I Will Always Love You” and “Killing in the Name”. (Admit it: if you’d had to guess which pair of songs came out on the same day, you wouldn’t have guessed those two.) However, I only know what month “I’m Not in Love” was released in; there’s a 1 in 31 chance that it was released on 1975-03-03, which would give it the exact same release date as “That’s the Way of the World”.

  • Currently, this list’s only songs that aren’t primarily in English are “Misirlou”, “Feels So Good”, “Axel F” (all instrumental), „99 Luftballons“ (German), and 「葬列」 (Japanese), though “Golden” has a few lines in Korean, and “Bohemian Rhapsody” famously includes some Italian and Arabic. (I may be missing some foreign lyrics in a few other songs; I haven’t gone down every song on the list yet.) The only non-English song titles are “Misirlou” (Greek), „99 Luftballons“ (German), and 「葬列」 (Japanese).

    • “Misirlou” is a romanization of «Μισιρλού», literally meaning roughly Egyptian Woman; it’s the feminine form of the surname Μισιρλής (Misirlés), meaning Egyptian, which ultimately descends from the Arabic مِصر (Miṣr) by way of the Turkish Mısır, which both mean Egypt. (Many surnames ending in -ου are exclusively feminine, but some are mixed-gender: one example of the latter is Χριστοδούλου [Christodoúlou, lit. servant of Christ]. Examples: the football player Christos, the composer Chris [whose Greek name is actually also Χριστός, but he romanizes it as Chris, perhaps to reduce the odds of Internet searches for either of them returning results for the other], and the economist Christodoulos. [Not everyone with the surname has an alliterative name, but the others aren’t as fun to say.])
    • „99 Luftballons“ is German for “99 Air Balloons”. It was later re-recorded in English as “99 Red Balloons”, but even in the United States, the German version is almost universally seen as superior.
    • 「葬列」 (｢Sōretsu｣) means “Funeral Procession”. The rōmaji of 椎名 林檎 is Shīna Ringo (Ringo Shīna in Western order). Her real name is 椎名 裕美子 (Shīna Yumiko); her stage name refers to her childhood nickname, 林檎 (ringo, meaning apple, from her habit of blushing), and to the Beatles’ Ringo Starr.

    Edge case: “Hallelujah” ultimately descends from the Hebrew הַלְלוּיָהּ (hal'luyáh, literally Praise Yah[weh]). I didn’t intentionally exclude songs in other languages, but by its very nature, this list requires songs to have huge commercial impacts in the United States or to have made exceptional impacts on me, and very few songs in foreign languages have ever made much commercial impact in the United States.

  • I made several omissions I regret (Chris Stapleton & Justin Timberlake’s “Tennessee Whiskey”/“Drink You Away”, Hozier’s “Take Me to Church”, and the Velvet Underground’s “All Tomorrow’s Parties” come to mind immediately), and if I spent longer thinking about it, I’d undoubtedly change several artists’ entries on this list (I went back and forth on what Michael Jackson and Madonna songs to list about a dozen times each), but I’ve overthought this list long enough as it is. Ironically, it’s nowhere near as perfect as any of the songs I listed, and I’ll just have to live with that.
