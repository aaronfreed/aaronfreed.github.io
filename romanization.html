Greek is one of the world’s oldest living languages, and as a result, it has undergone numerous pronunciation shifts over the course of its existence, which makes romanizing it a somewhat complex task. I will be focusing primarily on Attic Greek, the dialect spoken in Athens from 500 BCE-300 BCE, since most of the most important works of Greek philosophy date to that time. However, I will occasionally note shifts in other dialects of Classical Greek (also 500 BCE-300 BCE), Koinḕ Greek (300 BCE-400 CE), and Modern Greek (which can refer to any form of the language spoken since the 18th century, although in practice, it usually refers to Demotic Greek or Standard Modern Greek, which became Greece’s official language in 1976).

Weird exceptions

The lowercase letter sigma is written as ς if it is the last letter of a word, and σ in all other cases. Meanwhile, a few consonants change romanizations in certain cases:

  • γγ becomes ng
  • ρρ becomes rrh
  • ρ becomes rh at the start of a word

Diacritics

Pitch (Ancient only) and s
Stress (Modern only)

Both ancient Greek and its romanizations use three diacritics to mark pitch. Modern Greek only uses one of these, and for a different reason.

  1. The oxia, or acute accent (ά, έ, á, é, etc.), in ancient Greek, denotes a syllable pronounced with a high pitch. (In Modern Greek, it instead denotes stressed syllables.)
  2. The perispomeini, usually written as a circumflex accent (ᾶ, ῆ, â, ê, etc.), indicates a syllable pronounced with a falling pitch. (The perispomeini was evidently not used over the letter ε.)
  3. The varia, or grave accent (ὰ, ὲ, à, è, etc.), indicates a syllable pronounced with a lower pitch. (No, this is not where Nintendo got the name of the Metroid suit; it was a mistranslation of “Barrier” that sounded cool, so it stuck.)

Vowel length (Ancient only)

Both ancient Greek and its romanizations use two diacritics to mark vowel length:

  1. Macrons (ᾱ, ῑ, ā, ē, etc.) indicate long vowel sounds.
  2. The vrachy, or breve (ᾰ, ῐ, ă, ĕ, etc.), if present, indicates a short vowel sound.
    • If a romanized vowel uses neither a breve nor a macron, it’s probably short.
    • Epsilon (Ε, ε), eta (Η, η), omicron (Ο, ο), and omega (Ω, ω) are traditionally romanized as, respectively, e, ē, o, and ō.

Breathing markers (Ancient only)

The following diacritics occur in Ancient Greek, but not in romanizations thereof:

  • The psili, or smooth breathing marker (ἀ, ἐ, ἠ, etc.), indicates the absence of a voiceless glottal fricative before a vowel sound. Modern Greek omits this marker because it’s a uniform rule.
  • The dasia, or rough breathing marker (ἁ, ἑ, ἡ, etc.), indicates a voiceless glottal fricative, /h/. This usually occurs at the start of a word, and only occurs over vowels or the letter rho (Ρρ). Rho and upsilon (Υυ) always take rough breathing when they begin a word; additionally, when a word contains two successive rhos, the first one takes smooth breathing and the second takes rough breathing (e.g., διάῤῥοια, diárrhoia). In ancient Greek, the dasia was called δασὺ πνεῦμα (dasù pneûma, literally bare breath); in Latin, it was called spīritus asper (rough breath). Modern Greek does not contain this sound and therefore never uses this marker.
  • The coronis marks a vowel contracted by crasis, or inter-word contraction of a diphthong: e.g., κἀγώ (kagṓ) or κᾱ̓γώ (kāgṓ), a contraction of καὶ ἐγώ (kaí egṓ, and I). Unlike the psili (to which it is visually identical), a coronis may occur inside a word.

Note: I didn’t misspell diphthong, and it’s correctly pronounced difthong. Its hideous spelling is brought to you by the Attic pronunciation of δίφθογγος [having two sounds]: /dípʰ.tʰoŋ.ɡos/. Thus, we’re stuck with diphthong. Koinḕ [/ˈðiɸ.θoŋ.ɡos/] and Byzantine [/ˈðif.θoŋ.ɡos/] both simplified its pronunciation, but English orthography is extraordinarily conservative (and thus lacks any sort of consistency standards, or, more succinctly, doesn’t exist), so we’re not allowed to have nice things. (It’s quite ironic that English is so innovative in most other respects, but its orthography is so conservative. But I digress.)

Diaresis (Modern only)

The following diacritic occurs in Modern Greek, but not in romanizations thereof:

  • A diaresis, or umlaut, indicates a diph­thong.

Pronunciation

Vowels

Classical Latin and romanized Attic Greek use identical vowel sounds, thus:

  • ă is pronounced /a/, as in what
  • ā is pronounced /aː/, as in father
  • ĕ before a consonant is pronounced [ɛ], as in pet
  • ĕ before a vowel is pronounced [e], as in may
  • ē is pronounced [eː], as in outré
  • ĭ before a consonant is pronounced [ɪ], as in sit (in most accents)
  • ĭ before a vowel is pronounced [i], as in free
  • ī is pronounced [iː], as in machine
  • ŏ is pronounced [ɔ], as in sort
  • ō is pronounced [oː], as in holy
  • ŭ before a consonant is pronounced [ʊ], as in book
  • ŭ before a vowel is pronounced [u], in boot
  • ū is pronounced [uː], as in true
  • y̆ before a consonant is pronounced [ʏ], as in German Stück
  • y̆ before a vowel is pronounced [ʏ], as in schützen
  • ȳ is pronounced [yː], as in German früh
  • ae is pronounced /ae̯/, like y in fly
  • au is pronounced /au̯/, like ou in doubt
  • ei is pronounced /ei̯/, like ey in hey
  • eu is pronounced /eu̯/, like ow in the British pronunciation of low
  • oe is pronounced /oe̯/, like oy in destroy
  • oi is pronounced like oi in android
  • ui is pronounced /ui̯/, like ooey in phooey
  • yi is pronounced like wi in wit

Consonants

Classical Latin consonants’ pronunciation resembles modern English pronunciation, with the following major exceptions:

  • c is always pronounced as in cat
  • ch is always pronounced as in chemistry
    • Almost always a romanization of χ in a Greek loanword (this is also true of most ch combinations that English pronounces as k; e.g., chemistry descends from χυμεία, and Christ from Χρῑστός)
  • g is always pronounced as in good
  • gn is always pronounced like ngn in wingnut
  • j is always pronounced like y in yellow
    • Classical Latin usually wrote this i, but modern transcriptions often use j to indicate that it was used as a consonant (usually as the first letter of a word)
  • ph is always pronounced like p in party
    • Almost always a romanization of φ in a Greek loanword (this is also true in English)
  • qu is only ever pronounced as in quick, except when it precedes i, when it is pronounced as in French cuir
  • r is rolled as in Italian and Spanish
  • rh is likewise rolled, but with an extra h sound thrown in
    • Almost always a romanization of ρ in a Greek loanword (this is also true in English)
  • s is only ever pronounced as in say
  • th is pronounced as in thigh.
    • Almost always a romanization of θ in a Greek loanword (this is less consistently true in English)
  • v, when it is used as a consonant, is pronounced like w in wine, not v as in vine
    • In Classical Latin, u and v were the same letter; indeed, calling V its majuscule and u its minuscule is barely even oversimplifying. However, modern transcriptions usually separate them

Ecclesiastical Latin pronunciation is mostly based on modern Italian pronunciation. Thus:

  • c in cae, ce, ci, coe, and cy is pronounced like ch in change
  • c in ca, co, and cu is pronounced like c in cat
  • ch is pronounced like c in cat
