  14. Aaron Freed – “Dissolvēre populum ac ēligere alium” (5:20) [DR13]

    Latin: “To Dissolve the People and Elect Another”

    Used on: #25 “Sea of Slime”

    Named after an immortal quote from German playwright Bertolt Brecht. This song itself is one of two songs inspired by David Wise’s “Aquatic Ambiance”; its piano and strings are also inspired by Radiohead’s “Glass Eyes”. It’s also one of the rare cases I used a Steinway; I generally prefer either the Yamaha C7 (for its brighter sound) or Bechstein (for its clearer note separation). –Aaron

  23. Aaron Freed – «Malizia premeditata» (10:00) [DR12]

    Italian: “Malice Aforethought”

    Used on:

    • #47 “Breathing Nothing at All”
    • #65 “Carpe *mumble mumble Latin*”
    • #77 “There’s No Place Like Up” (partial)
    • #78 “It’s Not *My* Brain” (partial)

    One of several repurposed songs originally written for another project. It came together very quickly and uses a grand total of two instruments: Yamaha CS-80 and Roland TR-808. (OK, fine, Cherry Audio’s GX-80 and Logic Pro’s TR-808 samples. What do I look like, Uncle Moneybags over here?) I’m really proud of the drum pattern, which consists of alternating measures of 𝟕
    𝟒    , 𝟏𝟑
    𝟖    , 𝟕
    𝟒    , and 𝟏𝟓
    𝟖    . It’s extraordinarily disorienting and a major component of this track’s nightmarish feel.

    This track became effectively the Tycho plank’s main theme («Κοιμητήριον» [“Graveyard”], one of my later tracks, uses the exact same drum pattern at a faster tempo), so we wound up using snippets of it in several levels. YouTube audience retention data and listener feedback suggest it’s my most popular Rubicon track and one of its most popular by any composer. I wasn’t expecting such an avant-garde track to be so popular, but I’m certainly not complaining. Perhaps we used it so often it got stuck in players’ heads, though I don’t entirely understand how that’s possible. –Aaron

  33. Aaron Freed – “Ambiēns aquātica” (6:40) [DR14]

    Latin: “Aquatic Ambiance”

    Used on:

    • #06 “Blasted Vent Cores”

    This is probably this soundtrack’s oldest song; I wrote, arranged, mixed, and mastered it in a single night over two years ago, having heard its namesake (David Wise’s similarly titled Donkey Kong Country track) a grand total of once. I set out to make a song with a similar atmosphere but more energy, and this was the result. I doubt I’d have felt comfortable writing such a close pastiche had I been more familiar with it, but since my recollection of the song was far from perfect, I knew it wouldn’t end up close enough to qualify as plagiarism.

    This track was a huge milestone in me finding my voice as a composer, so I owe Wise an enormous debt I doubt I can ever repay. It’s probably also a major reason Hank invited me to join this project to begin with: I think it was his favorite of my tracks until “A Deal with God”. –Aaron

  61. Aaron Freed – «Λᾱ́ζᾱρος ἐκ μηχανῆς» (5:00) [DR10]

    Attic Greek: “Lazarus from the Machine”
    Romanized: «Lā́zāros ek mekhanês»

    We originally planned on having no music on “Lazarus ex machina”, but we decided it needed music towards the end of development, and since its layout is so convoluted that I couldn’t fade out the music as players fade out the climactic Ascension theme based on their location, I threw together this deliberately minimalistic drone track in about forty minutes (not an exaggeration).

    Utlimately, though, I thought of an approach that would enable us to fade out the music without having to care about the player’s location, so we ultimately reused “Hastur” on this level. I wasn’t really bothered by that, since I wrote this so quickly. For the soundtrack, we still left it in the position of “Lazarus ex machina”, which would otherwise have no track to represent it.

    The familiar Latin phrase Deus ex machina (God from the machine) is calqued from the far less familiar Greek phrase ἀπὸ μεχανῆς θεός (apò mēkhanês theós); as ex is a cognate of ἐκ (ek), I changed it accordingly. I also reordered the words to fit the more familiar Latin order; both orders are valid in both languages. (Languages with noun cases, like Latin and Greek, usually have more flexible word orders than languages without them, like English.)

    The titular Lazarus was a man Jesus resurrected in the New Testament. I’ll spare explaining how this relates to the level, since many people reading this page may not have played Rubicon yet. –Aaron
