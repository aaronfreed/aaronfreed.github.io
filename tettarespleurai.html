Introduction

I write a lot of music: from June 2024 to April 2025 alone, I wrote or cowrote over five hours of original music. This collection contains my four favorite tracks I worked on from August 2024 to June 2026 – one from each album I worked on during that time span. I’ve always loved old double LPs with one track to a side, so once I had that many side-length compositions, I felt obligated to collect them.

These tracks are, of course, atypical of my work in a few ways:

  • They’re about twelve minutes above my average track length. (It’s no coincidence that I think they’re all worth anthologizing: I only write songs that long if I think their quality justifies the length.)
  • «…καί αὐ­τοῦ μου­σι­κή ἦν ἠλ­εκ­τρι­κή» and “Om­nēs vi­ae Sō­lem dū­cunt” have vocal parts, which I rare­ly write.
  • While I’m known to insert brief quotes of other composers’ work as easter eggs, I rarely rewrite existing songs outright. But two of these tracks are heavily based on other composers’ work:
    • «…καί αὐ­τοῦ μου­σι­κή ἦν ἠλ­εκ­τρι­κή» interpolates four Chris Chris­to­dou­lou ⟨chrischristodoulou.bandcamp.com⟩ tracks (Risk of Rain series, Dead­bolt) with permission (for which I’m im­mense­ly grate­ful).
    • «𓈖𓐍𓅱𓏏𓀁 𓈖𓏏 𓉡𓁥» is partly based on Alex Se­ro­pi­an’s “Leela” (Ma­ra­thon, 1994-12-21) and Martin O’Don­nell & Michael Salvatori’s “Covenant Dance” (Halo: Combat Evolved, 2001-11-15). I began writing it for Eternal ⟨eternal.bungie.org⟩ and forgot to stop; in fact, I’m still not done.
      • I’ve included a previous mix (which lacks the current second movement and had a different third movement) in a bonus folder. I’m planning to move the old third movement to another level; it’s a perfectly fine piece of music, but it’s not a sufficiently climactic ending.

But they also showcase many hallmarks of my style for most/all of their runtime:

  • Eschewal of verse/chorus structure
  • Unorthodox chord progressions
  • Complex, fill-laden drum parts
  • Compound time signatures
  • Complex arrangements employing dense arpeggiation
  • Long, gradual, dramatic dynamic shifts
  • Mostly instrumental, with lyrics only in foreign languages
  • Foreign-language album/song/movement titles (Latin, Attic Greek, Middle Egyptian)

My music as a whole is too stylistically diverse to fit cleanly into one genre, but this anthology can reasonably be described as progressive rock/metal and/or post-rock. My biggest conscious influences are:

  1. Johann Sebastian Bach
  2. Brian Eno
  3. The Beatles
  4. Pink Floyd
  5. Nobuo Uematsu
  6. Vangelis
  7. King Crimson
  8. Genesis (and related solo acts, e.g. Phil Collins and Peter Gabriel)

My other in­flu­en­ces include Steve Al­bi­ni, Beas­tie Boys, Black Sa­bbath, Chris Chris­to­dou­lou, John Col­trane, Mi­les Da­vis, Death­spell Ome­ga, Bob Dylan, Tim & Geoff Fol­lin, Mar­vin Gaye, Phi­lip Glass, God­speed You! Black Em­pe­ror, Joy Di­vi­sion, Kō­en­ji Hy­ak­kei, Ma­gma, Me­tal­li­ca, Ya­su­no­ri Mit­su­da, Jan­elle Mo­náe, Jo­an­na New­som, Night­wish, Nine Inch Nails, Steve Reich, Ren, Rush, Rin­go Shī­na, Slint, Talk Talk, Talk­ing Heads, Tears for Fears, Tool, the Vel­vet Un­der­ground, Win­dir, Yes, Neil Young, & Frank Zap­pa. Some of these are un­doub­ted­ly more ob­vi­ous in­flu­en­ces than oth­ers: e.g., the Beas­tie Boys’ in­flu­ence on my mu­sic is like­ly quite ob­lique, but Paul’s Bou­tique has lived rent-free in my head for some­thing like twen­ty years, and while I’ll ne­ver in a mil­lion years im­it­ate Death­spell Ome­ga’s com­po­si­tion­al sty­le, they’re pro­bab­ly most re­spon­si­ble for my at­tempts to com­bine cho­ral mu­sic and me­tal.

    2026 commentary: By far my favorite piece from Compositions 2023-2024 ⟨aaronfreed.github.io/compositions2023-2024notes.html⟩, which I consider in many ways my first true album (outside juvenilia and cover/arrangement albums). Its first two movements, whose titles are Attic Greek translations of phrases from Risk of Rain 2, are based on several of Chris Christodoulou’s works from the Risk of Rain series; in fact, Chris himself encouraged me to write this piece, for which I owe him an immense debt that I doubt I can fully repay. It turned out better than I could’ve hoped.

    The first movement, whose title is an Attic Greek translation of the phrase Risk of Rain 2 displays when its optional boss Alloy Worship Unit activates, is based on Risk of Rain 2’s “The Raindrop That Fell to the Sky” (tied for my favorite song in any video game with Nobuo Uematsu‘s “Dancing Mad”). Alloy Worship Unit only spawns on Siren’s Call, one of the two levels that play “The Raindrop That Fell to the Sky”. I reharmonized the original melodies and wrote several new parts for this movement, so roughly half its songwriting is mine.

    I went ham for the second movement, bringing in elements of three other Risk of Rain songs: “They Might as Well Be Dead” and “Once in a Lullaby” from Survivors of the Void, and “Moisture Deficit” from the first Risk of Rain. (Note that “They Might as Well Be Dead” is a new arrangement of “Moisture Deficit”.) I had to pitch-shift and time-stretch them to fit with “The Raindrop That Fell to the Sky” synth, but once I did, they fit better than I could’ve hoped: there wasn’t any dissonance until I wanted it. Most of the instrumentation here comes from the Risk of Rain series, but I still wrote some new synth parts, and I’m partly responsible for the new chord progressions, so I claim about 25% of the second movement’s songwriting as my own. Its title is an Attic Greek translation of the message the game displays when you use a Lunar Seer for Sundered Grove, the stage that uses “They Might as Well Be Dead”.

    The second half wanders substantially further from Risk of Rain music while staying at least somewhat tied to its chord progressions. (However, those chord progressions at least partly result from me mashing up elements of different tracks, so they’re at least partly original to this work.)

    The third movement is named for John Coltrane’s A Love Supreme, and listeners have compared it to the bizarre French group Magma, one of my favorite bands (and one whose style I thought I‘d never successfully emulate. Chris clearly likes them as well: Seekers of the Storm’s “Tlālōcān” is a clear pastiche of their “De Futura”). The lyrics are taken from the Trisagion, a prayer traditionally recited three times in both Greek and Latin. I took liberties in swapping the final two lines’ traditional order, as the rhythm worked better that way. (The vocal parts were the last thing I wrote for this movement, and the last thing I added besides the brief quote of Nine Inch Nails’ “The Downward Spiral” as a nod to the track’s original title.)

    The final movement’s title is an Attic Greek calque of “Diēs Īrae”, whence its lyrics. (I did work several musical references to its famous first line into my arrangement, but the main melody is my own.) I rarely write vocal parts, but I felt this piece needed some, and I was immensely pleased with how they turned out. I almost felt like I’d channeled Johann Sebastian Bach, and I rarely feel that way. I was also quite pleased with the guitar solo; I think it’s the best one I wrote for Compositions 2023-2024.

    The song title itself is my Attic Greek translation of a phrase heavily associated with Chris since the first Risk of Rain game. It’s the in-game description of the series’ iconic Ukulele item, which randomly shocks several enemies when you hit one. And to answer an inevitable question: yes, the Greek titles are also partly in tribute to Chris (as well as to Vangelis, an immense influence on us both). I used Attic instead of Modern Greek because I know the former way better and wanted these titles to be grammatically correct.

    My use of religious lyrics here is not meant to convey any personal religious beliefs: I have none to convey. They simply felt appropriate for the song’s atmosphere.

    Key signature: E Dorian, and/or all of them.

    OK, John Coltrane’s “Giant Steps” (which travels through B major, G major, and E♭ major in rapid succession) might still take the cake, but this is still probably almost as tonally ambiguous as the Beach Boys’ “God Only Knows” (which infamously can be read as either A major or E major).

    We start on an E minor chord, but the first three chords alone make it ambiguous whether we’re in E natural minor or E Dorian: its second chord, C♯m7(♭5), is non-diatonic to E minor, while its third chord, Cmaj7, is non-diatonic to E Dorian. After another E minor chord, we see C♯m (non-diatonic to E minor), C major (non-diatonic to E Dorian), and A major (non-diatonic to E minor) just in the next few measures, but an A major-E minor plagal cadence does suggest we’re in E Dorian.

    Overall, later parts of the song conform to several diatonic scales, including B major, A major, and E♭ major, but it never stays true to any of them more than a handful of measures; however, at the end of the song’s 42-measure-long chord progression, a B major chord resolves to E minor and we restart our progression. This very clear V–i resolution makes it obvious that our tonal center contains the E minor chord, which is why I ultimately classify the song’s key signature as both “E Dorian” and “All of them”.

    2026 commentary: This was a musical expression of my immense despondency after a catastrophic betrayal by several people I’d considered friends. It began as a pastiche of “Castle of a Silence”, a brilliant Yoshitaka Hirota track from Shadow Hearts (an immensely underrated JRPG, by the way), but as it continued, it grew into something more, influenced by Yasunori Mitsuda’s “Prisoners of Fate” (Chrono Cross), Maurice Ravel’s “Boléro”, Nobuo Uematsu’s “Anxious Heart” (Final Fantasy VII), and the Beatles’ “Eleanor Rigby” (Revolver). Once it became clear that this would be a twenty-minute epic, I inevitably wrote a metal climax. Again, I’m pleased with how the guitar solo turned out, and this became my favorite track from my second album, Mūsica ex tempore malōrum ⟨aaronfreed.github.io/musicaextemporemalorum.html⟩, despite the dire circumstances surrounding its creation.

    The title is Attic Greek for “House of Closed Windows, Castle of Silence”. Its first half is a translation of the Japanese title of “Castle of a Silence”. I also made a negative harmony version of this, naturally titled «Οἶκος ἀνοικτῶν θυρίδων, ἀκρόπολις ὔμνων» (“House of Open Windows, Castle of Songs”). It appears on my third album, Κατηγορική ἀπολογίᾱ ⟨aaronfreed.github.io/kategorikeapologia.html⟩, and on my anthology Iānuāriō Aprīlem MMXXV ⟨aaronfreed.github.io/ianuarioaprilemmmxxv.html⟩ (Latin: January-April 2025). It makes the influence from “Boléro” vastly more obvious and everyone else’s influence vastly less obvious.

    This is my fourth-favorite song I’ve ever written. Bluntly, that means it’s my least favorite of these four tracks. I’ve put considerably more work into this anthology’s other three tracks, and I think it shows. However, I also think that’s more a reflection of its surrounding circumstances than of the quality of its songwriting – there’s nothing wrong with the underlying chord progressions, melodies, or arrangements. I’m especially pleased with how its string parts and voice leading worked out. It’s just missing a certain level of polish I was able to give this anthology’s other three tracks. I’d already considered going back and revising it to provide that polish, and writing this has made me consider that even more strongly.

    It says a lot that I even rank this as my fourth-best track, given its surrounding circumstances. Even listening to around half of Mūsica ex tempore malōrum still causes me to feel an all-consuming rage. It’s no coincidence that many of its tracks are still in a state that I consider fundamentally unfinished (though ADHD is another factor in that). It helps that I link this track to the healing after the betrayal, while I link several of its contemporaries directly to the betrayal itself. As a result, this track doesn’t taste like ash when I listen to it.

    We briefly considered this track for the Rubicon soundtrack, but there wasn’t really anywhere to put it, because none of the other three composers actually wrote orchestral works, so it felt quite out of place. If I ever finish my Marathon 2 soundtrack, it’ll go on “Kill Your Television” with a dynamic script to add new layers each time you pass another milestone (regardless of what order you pass them in). This is actually the exact reason it’s one of the rare tracks I’ve written that ends on a fadeout: it’s meant to loop endlessly. Once you finish the level’s mission, the plan is to fade out everything except the strings.

    2026 commentary: The last song on my third album, Κατηγορική ἀπολογίᾱ ⟨aaronfreed.github.io/kategorikeapologia.html⟩ (romanized: Katēgorikḗ apologíā), is a rare case of a track I wrote for a specific level: “All Roads Lead to Sol…”, its namesake from Marathon 2. The title and first two stanzas of its lyrics are in Classical Latin because the phrase “All Roads Lead to Rome” came to us from Classical Latin (“Omnēs viae Rōmam dūcunt”).

    Despite all that, it actually started life as two separate compositions before I realized they’d actually work better together. I began the first movement as a pastiche of Godspeed You! Black Emperor, one of my favorite bands of all time. (Its title is Latin for “God’s Speed to You, Black Emperor”.) However, I couldn’t figure out how to continue it and put it aside as a problem to solve another day.

    That day came when I began writing a pastiche of Nightwish’s “The Greatest Show on Earth” and realized they were both in G minor. There was nothing for it but to connect the two pieces. At that point, I was able to continue the first movement by pastiching the Beatles’ “I Want You (She’s So Heavy)” on the grounds that:

    1. It perfectly fit the movement’s tempo and atmosphere.
    2. No one ever made a song worse by ripping off the Beatles.

    I wrote some electric violin parts as a transition to the next movement. The connective passage wound up feeling vaguely Chinese to me, though I have absolutely no idea why it feels that way to me beyond vibes. (I need to learn more about world music.)

    The Nightwish pastiche, in keeping with the goal of this being a Godspeed You! Black Emperor pastiche, builds steadily as a theme and variation of a simple chord progression and melody from Nightwish’s track for around five minutes. The second movement’s title is Latin for “4.6 Billion Years”, a reference to the age of the Earth. (Nightwish’s first movement is called “4.6”.)

    The final movement changes to a new chord progression while at first continuing to build. However, I realized I needed a more dramatic climax, so I first had most of the instruments drop out for a bit, then built them back up. I added a violin solo and two verses of lyrics in Latin, but I still felt something was missing. So I changed the chord progression again and added a guitar solo and a third set of verses in Attic Greek.

    The results exceeded my expectations in every way. No one will ever compare my Latin to Virgil or Cicero’s, nor my Greek to Homer or Xenophon’s, but by writing in foreign languages in which I’m far from fluent, I achieved degrees of poetic imagery and concision I can’t achieve in English. (You’re saying Virgil and Cicero wrong, by the way: they’re pronounced roughly “WEAR-gill” and “Kick-KAY-row” in Classical Latin.)

    The third movement‘s title is Latin for “I’ve Seen Things You People Wouldn’t Believe”, the opening line of Roy Batty (Rutger Hauer)’s famous “tears in rain” soliloquy. Hauer, who cowrote the soliloquy, passed away in 2019, and I felt it a fitting tribute. The first stanza’s second through fourth lines also refer to the soliloquy.

    Other influences on the lyrics include the original Marathon trilogy and a few of its fan games, in particular Rubicon (for which I cowrote a soundtrack) and Eternal (which I’ve worked on since 2018). I also snuck in references to Star Trek: Deep Space Nine’s “In the Pale Moonlight” and Ren’s “Hi Ren”, the latter of which has very similar themes to Eternal. Almost the entire third stanza is based on a secret terminal from Marathon 2’s “Kill Your Television” that also heavily inspired Eternal.

    2026 commentary: For the most part, this may have been the most effortless set of melodies and harmonies I’ve ever written. I began this as a vamp on “Covenant Dance” and “Leela” and it turned into so much more than that. I began with a forty-eight-measure set of chord progressions that were mostly based on ascending or descending bass lines, all of which came naturally to me, and over them I wrote some of the most effortless and elegant melodies I’ve ever written, again with almost no effort.

    That wasn’t enough, so I added another forty-eight-measure set of chord progressions with more melodies, most of which also came effortlessly to me. This progression also modulated down by a whole tone, since an earlier sixteen-measure progression in the song had modulated up by a whole tone, and I wanted to contradict musical convention by using a modulation down to raise tension.

    That happens in the form of the inevitable metal section, which moves the original synth melodies to a Les Paul while adding new melodies on top of them. This was where my inspiration initially ran dry: I couldn’t figure out what to do over the inevitable “Diēs Īrae” quotation, so in original releases of the song, the synth dropped out from that point forward.

    Regardless, effectively everyone that heard what I’d written agreed it was a banger. Talashar told me it sounded like the Black Mages doing Bungie music, and ever since, I’ve never been able to unhear it. We wound up using this track’s first and third movements as the level music for “Wading in Vitriol”, the action-packed final level of Rubicon’s Pfhor plank. It will also appear in Eternal’s “Dark Grotto of the Lethe” after Nicholas Singer’s own brilliant “Leela / Covenant Dance” mash-up.

    I ultimately did finish those synth parts several months later, again more or less effortlessly once I resumed writing for them. You’ll hear them in this mix. The only real problem I’ve had is that the Logic project file for this track has gotten so dense and complex that it takes roughly fifteen minutes to export, and my poor computer often can’t even play parts of it in real time, so I have to export it to hear everything in context.

    I gave the first movement a Finnish title meaning “Hathor’s Lament / Leela / Dance of the Covenant” since it is a sterling example of progressive metal, and Finland has more metal bands per capita than any other country. I don’t actually speak Finnish, but the grammar here is simple enough (at least for those familiar with Latin, Greek, or German) that I don’t need to: it only uses the genitive and nominative cases.

    The second movement’s title is Japanese for “Hathor’s Lament / Leela / Covenant Dance of the Ominous Star”. The Japanese title of Nobuo Uematsu’s “Dancing Mad” means roughly “Wild Dance of the Ominous Star”, “Chaotic Dance of the Calamitous Star”, or “Rebellious Dance of the Bewitching Star”. This movement is heavily inspired by its chord progressions, so making a pun on its title felt like the thing to do. This movement’s melodies again came to me more or less effortlessly; the only time-consuming part has been some of the arrangements. I currently consider this movement the least complete of the three, and that’s why. I may also expand it a little bit, since some of its ideas feel like they barely have time to establish themselves.

    The third movement is the most directly based on “Leela” (which is panned toward the left channel) and “Covenant Dance” (which is panned toward the right). I altered the latter’s timing a bit because “Leela” uses five-measure phrases, while “Covenant Dance” uses four-measure phrases. With that change in place, they harmonize together beautifully, with only occasional dissonance.

    The movement title is Attic Greek for “Hathor’s Lament / Leela / Dance of the Covenant”. I used Attic Greek because of the concept of the Greek chorus. Given how choral this movement is, it felt appropriate.

    This is the best track I’ve ever (mostly) written. I don’t think there’s even a question about that. Its title is grammatically questionable Middle Egyptian for “Hathor’s Lament”. It’s pronounced roughly “Nekhwoot net Het-HEY-roo”, with the kh pronounced like the ch in Scottish loch or German Bach. At least, we think so. Hieroglyphs give us little to no indication what the vowel sounds were, so some of it is just guesswork.

    I gave it a Middle Egyptian title because its namesake, Eternal’s Hathor, is named for an Egyptian goddess. I plan to use variants of this as her theme throughout the game – I’m that pleased with it.

    This is the last track on my fourth album, Τὸν ὦνον πάντων καί τὴν ἀξίᾱν οὐδένων ⟨aaronfreed.github.io/tonononpanton.html⟩ (Attic Greek for The Price of Everything and the Value of Nothing). I knew I wasn’t going to top it anytime soon, so that seemed like the perfect place to stop writing songs for the album. Tracks written after this one took shape have gone on my in-progress fifth album, Āleātōrium semper vincit ⟨aaronfreed.github.io/aleatoriumsempervincit.html⟩.
